Precepts of Tonal Magic

by Pania Cadiusus

[pagebreak]

<p align="left">
<img src='img://Textures/Interface/Books/Illuminated_Letters/T_letter.png'>he capacity to form magickal effects by application of resonance and harmony was not unique to the Dwemer, nor can it be guessed to have been their invention, but none can doubt that they were the masters of the craft. 

A quote sometimes ascribed to Dumac, King of the Dwemer in the time of the First Council, when he was confronted by Nerevar, was:
"Stand down, Nerevar, or in the name of the Fifteen-and-One Golden Tones, I will smite you".

Numerologists have been quick to assign this number, fifteen-and-one, to the Daedric princes. It certainly cannot be discounted, although the Dwemer themselves had little patience for any beings that considered themselves divine. What is possible is that these 'Tones' were emanations that represented those realms of Oblivion - or even that the Dwemer simply regarded the Princes as noises to be manipulated.

But why Fifteen-and-one, and not Sixteen? The answer lies in traditional worship of the Divines, who viewed the Aedra not as 'Nine' but as Eight plus One - the one being Talos, whose presence in Aetherius had not always been the case, but also acted as a metaphysical balance, axis or centre.

As for which being might be considered the 'One' in the Dwemer worldview, it is unknown. What noises were ascribed to the realms of Oblivion, if any at all? To determine that, we must look at the Princes themselves. But first there is the matter of AE.

In ancient Yokudan folklore, Anu is called Satak, but is also referred to as 'The Hum', a 'force so prevalent as to be not really there at all'. If one spends enough time in a Dwemer ruin on Vvardenfell, the humming noise is perpetual - and while migraine-inducing at first, after a while it only becomes audible when you can longer hear it.

Scholars in Skyrim have noted that some ruins have an 'Aedrome', which seems to create something like this vibration. In Morrowind, the ruins seem to produce it themselves - usually this is put down to proximity to Red Mountain.

The prevalence of this background harmonisation cannot be a mistake or a coincidence. It is there deliberately, but why? I hypothesise that it acts as a 'canvas', or backdrop on which the other tones work. Whether or not this is one of the Golden Tones, I cannot say - or if it is even the 'One'.

While I was working in Morrowind with my father many years ago, I met the Nerevarine. The Nerevarine famously uncovered one of the profane tools of the Dwemer, the sound-chisel 'Keening'. The Songs of King Wulfharth claim that this weapon was made of the 'Sound of the Shadow of the Moons'. It is difficult to take anything from ancient Nordic myth at face value, but the description is compelling because it could refer to one of two things - the dead god Lorkhan (whose ghost the Khajiit say appears when the shadow-moon appears), and Azura (who lives in the Oblivion realm of Moonshadow).

Do the other princes present themselves as noises that the Dwemer could have used? It is tempting to think so. Dagon, I imagine as a loud and clamorous noise. Nocturnal I would take to be a quiet sussurus. But this is conjecture.

In order to truly determine the nature of the sacred tones, we would have to treat the Daedric Princes in the same way that the Dwemer did. This would be an error, since what became of the Dwarves can only have been due to their hubris in the face of the gods, whether by the retaliation of the divine forces or prolonged dalliance with forces that they could not truly comprehend.
