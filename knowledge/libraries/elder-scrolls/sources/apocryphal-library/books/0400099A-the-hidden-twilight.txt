The Hidden Twilight

by Carillius Melfus


</p>
[pagebreak]
<p align="left">
<img src='img://Textures/Interface/Books/Illuminated_Letters/T_letter.png'>he City of Inkseeds rose from the desert, shining and decadent. Somehow, it still stood. I crossed through the gate, and the beast knew exactly where to take me: the way worn by beggars and poets. The only place a man of my appetites can find satisfaction. I'm not proud, but then, nobody ever is.

I passed then, at the Senche-Tiger's guidance, the foot of the Tower of Moons, which was not the first name of that spire, but rather the one given to it by the Ragged Queen Dra'Zhari in the first days when the Eclipse Theatre settled the ruins forsaken by all others. Even arriving at the sand-choked gates was a journey unmade, unmakeable by any without the knowledge or the wit to decrypt it. 
As the Tower departed behind us, the hulking Tiger led further me through those haunted streets, the plaintive sounds of laughter and pipe-play danced upon the breeze, half-snatched melodies amid the desert winds, of the Eclipse Players performing old forbidden histories behind closed doors and curtains where they would not be interrupted by the sun.

At length I was brought to the Palace of Honey, decaying in its forgotten grandeur, where the greatest works in Tamrielic literature were inspired, or so it is said. The courtiers bowed with the same practiced elegance of those in the Imperial City and of High Rock, though they were dressed all in rags and the Palace itself still bore the disrepair of the ages.

To be allowed to sample the Skooma of the Palace of Honey is one of the rarest honours a dreamer can receive; it cannot be exported, and none who idly wander the Ne Quin'al will ever find their way. It is uncertain how it comes to pass that one is invited here - certainly I do not remember quite how I came to be offered the wisdom to bring me here. Perhaps I had been seeking it, despite its rarity - I have sampled many strange and elusive drugs during my life, and each brought a new satisfaction that could never quite be matched. In Morrowind, I indulged in a peculiar mixture, partly Skooma and partly some other strange medicine of the land, which the Dark Elves use to commune with their ancestors, their God-Kings, and the seven Daedra for whom they bear various reverence or fear. After those dreams, I wrote solidly for eight days and formed a poem which would always bring sorrowful delight to the dying, jubilant grief to those returning from war, and disastrous luck to gamblers.
I drank a blend from Skyrim, delivered to me by a strange merchant who told me that it had been mixed with the blood of Arkay himself. When my dreams passed I had written a poem that was a recipe whose ingredients did not exist, but the reading of which would inspire hunger lasting a day and a night. I fell to sickness after that, and sought not to drink it again.
In Valenwood, I was allowed to partake in a private ceremony involving the consumption of a type of Skooma I cannot name, and after that - for a month at least - I could only write books that had already been written. I tried many other preparations, until one day I knew that I had been invited to the Palace of Honey.

And here I now stood, before being walked to a heap of tattered cushions and torn-silk rugs. The rag-hooded Suthay-raht chuckled and bade me recline, before pouring the thick syrup - I remember now how it glistened like the copper-clouds of a perfect sunrise - into the waiting Skooma pipe. He lit the candle beneath, and told me to wait for the steam to appear from the spout.

As I waited, I listened to the moans of pain and pleasure from the visionaries on their heaped beds. The Suthay-raht chuckled again. "Sangiin walks in this place, but he cannot give or take anything he does not already have. This city is not his. There see, now the steam rises. May Azurah bring you great sight, traveller."

I inhaled from the pipe, and first noticed the wash of colour spread across my vision. I lay down on the bed of torn splendour, and noticed the faces of the other partakers around me. There were Khajiit, but also I saw Elves and Men among them too. I rolled onto my back, the colours swirling into each other, and reached for the pipe again, taking another draught. Above me, painted onto the ceiling, was an image of the moons, Jone and Jode for so the Khajiit call them. I had observed the moons many times in many a stupor, seen the dance of Masser and Secunda across the heavens. I knew that in the reaches of Elsweyr, the position of the moons was profound indeed, affecting the forms that the Khajiit take at birth, from the Suthay-raht who had lit my Skooma pipe to the Senche Tiger who had guided me here, to the Cathay woman who ruled the city from atop the Tower of the Moons.

I pondered the panoply of the heavens as I smoked idly at the pipe, each draught bringing with it further musings on the intricacies the moon and stars above. I recalled the various notions of what the moons truly are; are they the body of Lorkhan, or are they ancient spirits who took part in the creation of Mundus? 

I sat up on the bed, taking the last long draught of the pipe. I noticed, then, that I was alone in the Palace. The other beds were empty, and the ragged-robed attendants were not in sight. I leaned forward from my crib of torn sheets to confirm that this was indeed the case. Puzzled, I stood and looked around more. The Palace was indeed completely empty. I began to wander around, looking at the ancient masonry and fading gold embossing and bas-reliefs, where the original depictions of temple and palace life had long since been worn away by the march of the aeons.

I leaned out of the window, and was surprised to see that it was almost dusk. I had only arrived at the Palace in the mid-morning, could I have really been indulging for so long?

I left the Palace the way I had entered, and gazed over the sprawling City of Inkseeds, the streets just as abandoned as the Palace. Rather than feel a sense of dread, I felt called down into the maze of bazaars and markets. Letting my feet lead the way, I heard very little - but still heard occasional snatches of laughter and pipe-music behind windows from which no light shone.

I turned my gaze to the heavens - and saw that the moons were almost in alignment over the sun. It was not dusk after all, but a double-eclipse! I hurried to the city gate - I felt compelled to stand in the Ne Quin-al and witness it there. I was almost at the gate when the last of the sunlight hid behind Jone and Jode. And then even the music and the wind died away, and it was the first time I have known the death of sound.

Through the gates, towards me, walked a Khajiit - his body and face completely wrapped in bandages and rags. Only when his feet touched the ground was there any noise in the whole of existence. 

The beggar stretched his hand out - it was as clear a command as any I had ever been given. But what could I give to this man? I reached to the purse at my side, and pulled from it an amulet I had been given. It was a garnet, cut into a simple diamond shape, hung on a brass chain. It wasn't valuable, but the beggar accepted it. He twirled it between his fingers, and then he unwrapped the bandages around his chest. They were filthy with dried blood, and he had to peel them back to reveal the sight that caused me to stagger back in fright - for this beggar's chest had a huge cavity within, in which there beat no heart. I could see the ribs broken around the cavity - this man could surely not be alive. And then he placed into this aperture the red diamond-shaped amulet, and having done so, wrapped the bandages back around. 

He then pointed up - and my eyes followed his direction, to the moons above. Behind Masser and Secunda was another moon - it had not been there before. I lost my balance at that moment, and when I gained my footing again I was no longer in the desert. Nor was the sky the same I had just been looking at. The landscape of ruined cities lay before me, with broken towers on floating islands in the sky. The stars above swirled around the moons, which were enormous now. 

The beggar floated above me, three times the size he had been before. His bandages trailed behind him, and no longer concealed his rotting carcass, the amulet now gleaming bright red in his chest. I looked upon his face, but there was only darkness there, save two points of light where his eyes might be. I turned to flee, but the beggar just laughed in a booming voice that commanded huge vines to burst from the ground. I staggered back from them, as they reeked of poisonous fumes, and I put my hand to my face, coughing. I turned, then, to face the Heartless Man. In a voice that came from everywhere, he said;

DAE HAL ALTADOON GUME LKHAN

I remember the words clearly now - I do not know what they translate to, but the meaning was clear. It would do no good to flee in this place. 

I asked the being where I was; it showed me, drifting through the air, bringing me alongside it. I looked up at an entirely different moon - was it a moon? Was it even round? My mind swam as I tried to reconcile what I saw, before realising I was looking down from the firmament at Tamriel. And then I knew, I knew that I was standing on the phantom moon.

Then he carried me into the stars, where I stood between the lights of Mundus and the inky blackness of Oblivion. The darkness rippled as the eyes of the Divines went past, like perfect points of being in a boiling ocean of annihilation. I felt strangely peaceful now, serenely protected by the weight of the distance between myself and anything and everything.

And then, he took me further back, so I could see the whole wheel of creation. All the stars and planets, a perfect ever-spinning disc. He raised his hand, and tilted the wheel so it became visible not from above, but from the side. I do not know why, but this struck a very real fear into me, and I begged the Moon-Wight not to show me more. It looked at me with those twin points of light, and drew the red diamond from its chest. It placed it in my hand, and then sent me hurtling down into the world again.

I did not scream as I fell. I simply let the darkness overtake me, as so many dreams had done before. And so it was that I awoke, my eyes looking once more at the painting of the moons on the ceiling. It was daylight outside, and the ragged-robed Khajiit were still attending to the other visitors. I felt invigorated after what I had seen, as though I had rested a thousand nights, and I had no trouble standing, as I often do after partaking in Skooma. I made my way to the attendant I had spoken to earlier, and when I asked he told me that no, there had been no eclipse today.

"It is rare, yes, to see the Hidden Twilight, the twice-eclipse, in one's visions. It is rare indeed. Tell me, did you see a man there? A man with a missing heart? Ah, do not answer - this one sees already that you did. Khajiit hopes you were generous to him, yes?"

Then he cackled and shuffled away.

Though I felt refreshed, I wanted to enjoy the city a little longer before I headed back to Rimmen, so I spent another couple of nights in the City of Inkseeds, where it is said that the finest works of literature are inspired. I will write this down for all to know, and perhaps I will be inspired to write a poem that will evoke all the things I had seen and known, and create all the things I could never make.

While there, I asked some of the locals how the local skooma is produced. Most shook their heads, saying that they did not know, but an old woman with patchy fur under her rags told me this:

"The sugar is mixed not with nightshade, no. It is mixed with ink. Not the kind you write with, though Khajiit supposes you could have done so once. The kind of ink that runs in source ever-flowing. Before things became myths, they could be seen, and so inspiration flowed like ink. We take that ink, and we mix it with the moon sugar."
I did not truly understand the mystic's words. Were they mere poetry? Or was she right in saying that all poems were real once?

I left the city then; I did not think any more good could come of my stay, and so I left by the same route by which I had arrived. That was five months ago, and I cannot remember anything about the path to the city at all, or how I had come to know it.

I still have the garnet amulet, though the more I think about it, the less certain I am about where I came to get it in the first place.
