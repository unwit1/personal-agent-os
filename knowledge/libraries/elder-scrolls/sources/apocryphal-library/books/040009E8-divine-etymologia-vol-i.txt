<img src='img://Textures/Interface/Books/Illuminated_Letters/S_letter.png'>ince the Second Era, the term 'Journalling Onus' or 'Journal-Onus' became popular thanks to the works of Phrastus of Elinhir. He did not invent the term, of course, attributing it himself to the Aldmeriad, arguably the first recorded work of writing in existence.

What he did not mention was the similarity between that phrase and the name of Julianos, the god of logic, reason and, most pertinently to this discourse, writing. Other later scholars have since seized on this omission and it is hard to determine, in the midst of the usual academic squabbling, who published first.

In this more liberal age, now that the stringencies of certain orthodox ways of deific reverence are fading into the past, it has become more popular for us to approach the once-forbidden topic of what some call 'Adanymicetics', which is to say, naming gods into existence. The thorough reader will most likely be familiar with the 'Sennidhar' argument, whereby the gods of mercy and labour were supposedly once conflated into a single entity.

So, is the Journal-Onus truly the root of blessed Julianos' origin? The heretical order known as the Brass Pyramid certainly believe that this is the case, and they will remind any that they are absolutely devoted to their god, and do not consider his potential origin to diminish his very real divinity in any way. Of course, it flies in the face of much of the orthodoxy who cleave to the notion that we as men were created by the gods, and not the reverse. They point to the sacrifices that the gods made for the world to exist as evidence, and rightly so - the 'gift-limbs' that solidify the cosmos would simply not exist if we had to imagine the gods retroactively.

Nevertheless, there is some support amongst metatheologians and theurgists for the notion that our language has shaped the way that the Aedra appear to us as individuals and cultures. This being the case, we shall inspect the other etymological roots of Julianos.

The most  obvious is that of Jhunal, the old Nordic interpretation of the divine. His worship lost most of its potence in the second era, and in the fourth is almost unheard of apart from a few library-cults and nomadic clever-folk in Skyrim. But how did they come to name him?

The answer may come from the language of ancient Atmora. Knowledge of this language is extremely hard to come by, and its runic script predates the comparatively modern runes supposedly demonstrated by Ysgramor or Jhunal, depending on which tales are believed.

Ancient Atmoran seems to have been a language restricted to a priestly caste, and was not spoken by the common folk. It has thus been proposed to have been divine in nature, which may afford us an explanation of Jhunal's name.

Archaeolinguists piecing together the ancient language have proposed the following possible roots:

JUNAHLAH (Jun-Ah-Lah), which would transliterate as 'King Hunter Magic', or more idiomatically, 'The King who Seeks Magic'.

JUNSELOVAAS (Jun-se-Lovaas), "King of Poetry". This one, while more fitting in its translation, has been considered too distant syllabically by some. 

YAHNEHLO (Yah-Neh-Lo) "Seek never (to) deceive". The most popular translation at current. 

None of these are, at first glance, particularly compelling - but when one accounts for linguistic drift they do become plausible and there is precedent for such within the science.

Ultimately, these are the only two extant pillars that support the theory at the moment. The now-distant figure of Vivec the 'god king' claimed that 'JHUNAL' was the true name of one of the planets visible in the night sky, but many of the warrior-poet's claims have been dismissed by more recent writers. It is most likely that he came upon the name during the attempted Nordic conquests of Skyrim in the First Era.
