<img src='img://Textures/Interface/Books/Illuminated_Letters/O_letter.png'>f the heirs who ruled Skyrim between the death of Ysgramor and the crowning of Harald, extremely little is known. Even the number is a matter of some debate; Harald is largely considered to be the thirteenth of that issue, but it is never made certain whether or not Ysgramor himself should rank first among them, or more as a 'High King Zero' who was never formalised as king.

Second Era skalds claim that after the death of Ysgramor, the mantle of leadership fell to Ylgar, but even at this moment in time he is still referred to as 'Harbinger' rather than King. The Harbinger title remains in place today as the leader of the Companions, a fellowship of warriors that no longer has any official bearing on Skyrim's politics. The first to use the title of King is unknown, although forgotten history may afford some clue as to which type of person.

With Harald's ascension, the Animal Totem religion of the ancient Atmoran faith gradually morphed into the faith associated with early Nords, that of the Divines - though different to the Cyrod faith, which in those times had itself not fully emerged. Kyne was - and indeed to some still is - the chief of the gods in this old religion, with the other Divines being crudely syncretised one way or another. It is unknown when the Imperial aspect of Akatosh replaces the roundly shunned Atmoran aspect of Alduin, though it appears to have happened after the reign of Wulfharth.

Nevertheless, this affords some insight into the faith of the Nords at the end of the Merethic. At the turn of the era, Kyne - or The Hawk - was venerated, but Alduin was not. The Dragon God is outright vilified. So why, then, are so many ancient Nordic ruins steepled with Draconic iconography?

All of this, plus many more archaeological investigations, points to the truth behind the 'Dragon War' that is slowly emerging from myth into established history. In this time, the Dragon God was apparently worshipped by a select cabal of extremely powerful priests who controlled the population with a tyrannic hold. Accounts from this time indicate that they were allies of, had control over, or were themselves controlled by actual dragons, chief among whom was Alduin himself.

Ysgramor does not appear to have brought the draconic aspect of the faith over from Atmora himself, nor is there any current indication that his sons did. But with subsequent boats bearing more troops and supplies from the frozen north, it is possible that the dragon faith was brought from that land alongside them. The mentions of the dragon faith existing in Atmora are largely lost to time, but they do exist.

Which leads us back to the heirs of Ysgramor. Were they the Priest-Kings of Alduin? It would certainly have granted them that power. The dragon has been considered a symbol of kingship in Cyrodiil since the Saint Alessia's time, and it is entirely plausible that this symbology passed down to Cyrodiil through Nedic contact with the Nords.

However, no direct link has been drawn between the temporal rule of early Skyrim and its spiritual rule. The centre of the Alduin Faith appears to have been the now-abandoned city of Bromjunaar, while the administrative centre is widely believed to have been Ysgramor's city of Windhelm. 

Were, then, the sons of Ysgramor in fact thrall to priests, rather than ranking among them? Or were the sons those who rose up against the dragon faith and banished them into obscurity?

This author favours the latter, for the simple reason that by the time Harald is crowned, he is already apparently following a royal issue. Would the Nords have accepted a royal line that participated in the enthrallment of their people? It seems unlikely.

Therefore, this author concludes that the Heirs of Ysgramor were those who ruled over a somewhat-united Skyrim and Atmora in a temporal manner, while coming to resist and defy the Dragon faith that filtered down from the latter.
