Filament and Filigree

by Jelketheris


</p>
[pagebreak]
<p align="left">
<img src='img://Textures/Interface/Books/Illuminated_Letters/I_letter.png'> stared at my reflection in the metal, wondering if my face had hardened to match my inner mood. I had been working the piece for days, and the forge's swelter was taking its toll. I always came to the metal shop when the dark swam over me, and today was no exception. In the midst of the city's workshops and tradehouses, I found a strange sense of calm in the clamour, but a few hours of work and I would always need to take leave of my labours.

The gilded mirror reflected nothing of my temperament, however. Mirrors are not normally the purview of smiths, requiring delicate alchemy and the application of mithril distillates and silver before one even thinks about the frame. But the client had been specific, and demanded the use of this unusual metal.

She said the metal was brass, and that I was not to work in any metal other than that which she provided. Given the heft of the wages she'd promised, I was in no mind to argue. The material was not like the brass you find in the ornamentation of merchant-class tables and fittings, but was similar enough in colour. Much of what I had been given was ingots, but there was some other pieces too, which I was instructed to melt down - strange shaped things, domed or like toothed wheels. There was even what seemed to be a pauldron.

I was astounded by the metallurgical properties of the metal - Some of the pieces, not the ingots, would produce a marvellous, harmonious ringing sound when struck. Other pieces would make a dull thud, and almost seem to absorb noise. If I made a plate-segment from it, it had very little interesting noise quality to it whatsoever, simply sounding like ordinary metal. 

The mirror itself was to be roughly the size of a doorway. A very large piece to be sure. The main reflective piece was simple enough - once it had been planished and evened out, it reflected rather well - not as well as a normal mirror, but I could clearly make out my own reflection. It stared back at me, showing nothing of my temper or the darkness within me, only the tiredness in my eyes.

The next part of the job was the frame - and this was to be the difficult part. It was very ornate, though in a strangely geometric fashion. There were large plates that went around the outside, and these would be simple enough to reproduce from the schematics, but the threads were like nothing I'd ever made before.

Always thicker than a human hair but thinner than a piece of string, the threads - of which I would need several large spools - were to be made of that same metal. It was a lot of effort to beat the material to the right thickness, and then spin it - as if I were Mephala herself - into that substance like golden thread. The process took weeks, and I wove yards and yards of that filament into the fissures and cracks between the metal plates. I bunched it, and moved it into patterns, until finally my work was done.

The next day, the client sent a wagon to pick up the mirror. I was paid my wright's wage and the wagon departed. Over the next few days, for want of work to do, I would walk around the city when my mood turned sour - and that was when I saw the warrior.

He was a Redguard man, clearly a sailor as well as a fighter. He beamed at me as I approached, having noticed my wandering eyes, and he hailed me over. I learned that his name was Razim, and he told me the tale of the blade he had by his side - which was what had caught my attention in the first place, because it was made of that same metal with which I'd made the mirror.

"Yes, sharp as a clannfear's tusk it is - and tough to wear down in any fight! Made by the ancient Dwarves, you see. They had a knack for working this strange metal. Only they knew how."

"The Dwarves? Well, that explains a lot! I had recently been commissioned to make a mirror of this same metal, and I'd never seen it before."

"A mirror! Why would someone want a mirror out of such a metal?"

I went on to explain the very unusual nature of the project, and his face hardened. He told me that a year ago, on the coast nearby, a ruin of the ancient Dwarves had been found. He suggested that maybe the woman who had paid me had found the material there. My expectations and my curiosity overcame me, and I suggested out loud that maybe I could find some answers there. Razim seemed keen to join me, and so we decided to learn what we could. It would keep the darkness of my old war-stories at bay, having something like this to keep me occupied.

We planned it for a couple of days, ensuring we had suitable hard-wearing leather armour, lockpicks and probes of various lengths, and rope enough that we could scale any pits. Razim explained to me the mysteries of the few Dwarven machines he had seen in the past, which were like living suits of armour, or metal spiders which walked like real animals. I opted to bring a heavy lump-hammer from the forge when we eventually headed off.

The entrance to the ruin was in a cave along the coast, and some way in, our way lit by our sealed lanterns, we found the great megalithic pillars that marked our entry. Half-open were the doors of that same brass, and inside the chasms and waterfalls spilled over ancient buildings, grey-stone with gleaming domes like the minarets of Sentinel.

There was enough of the metal here that I could make weapons and armour out of nothing but, if I could bring enough with me. I left a pile of plates and gears by the door, so that if I were to return unladen I could bring some back with me. Razim found a shield and slung it over his shoulder.

We delved into the ancient ruins, with great waterfalls coming down from the sea above - what chasms must lie below that the sea would drain down so far! - Until we rounded a corner into a large room. The lights were still lit - not like torches though, they gleamed white-yellow and hissed or hummed. From the little gaps in the workings, I could see that those strange filaments that I had made for the mirror existed in these old light fitting, made by a race extinct for thousands of years! All around there thudded the sounds of long-forgotten machines, wheels within wheels clanking and screeching in the name of their disappeared creators. And there upon a table was a heap of books and scrolls. What ancient lore, untouched by the aeons, lay here for nosy scholars?

It was Razim who heard it before I did, and the first I knew about it was a loud clang as a metal arm struck his shield. I spun around to see what appeared to be a man attacking him - but it was as if the top half were a man, and the lower half a sphere! And the whole being was made of brass, with no flesh in the gaps - an armoured sentry of the forgotten Dwarves! 

Razim held the being off, but couldn't damage it with his sword. The sharp blade was a boon against flesh, but little use against the Sphere, which was mostly a living piece of armour. And so he mostly angled his shield in such a way that the golem's blows simply glanced away, and on those few times he was hit, he managed to roll with the blows in such a way that he wasn't badly hurt. 

I readied my great hammer and struck the sphere, which certainly had a more profound effect, staggering the golem, but the iron of the hammer-head was insufficient to dent the sphere. While it was distracted, Razim darted aside and picked something up, sliding it across the floor to me - it was a great warhammer, of Dwarven brass!

I picked up the great hammer and delivered to the golem a series of well-timed blows. Working the forge had given me the stamina needed to launch many heavy blows in quickl succession without getting tired for a while. This was just what was needed, and together we had vanquished the Dwarven Sphere-Golem.

Razim then went over to look at the books while I inspected the remains of the foe. Behind the countenance of its eyes, a metal bearded face, was a mass of filigree of that same kind I had made. I realised the entire of its construction could fold into the sphere, allowing it to roll around like a ball until it needed to fight - and it was stabilised by a little wheel inside, that spun inside a greater wheel. Such incredible understanding of metal! I had not realised until now that the Dwarves were such experts in my field, experts to shame my own efforts.

There was a gemstone inside, also. I recognised it as a soul gem, and I wondered if the soul of a creature was what powered the golem. Did it allow it to fight as a thinking being? I could not answer that. It was then that Razim hollered, and so I pocketed the gem and walked on over. 

He had found among the scrolls and books various schematics, and it soon became clear that one of these was the same as that which I'd been working on - only then did I notice a spent torch on the table. She had been here, and copied the schematics, perhaps a whole year ago! As we looked over the scroll for some time, it became clear why she hadn't just taken them - they were just about too fragile to move far from the library, but not so much that we couldn't inspect them here. Neither of us could read the Dwemer language, though I recognised the letters well enough, as they were similar to Aldmeris runes but more triangular. The words seemed clumsy and devoid of vowels, nothing that made any sense. But the more we looked over them, and the other works, the more concerned we became. The pictograms and diagrams showed strange things - corpses coming to life, people becoming transparent, a huge metal statue that seemed to become armoured all at once. A heart struck with a hammer and a chisel, rendering everything around it translucent, or vanished? Without a better understanding of the glyphs, we didn't know what it meant, but we became very concerned about what I had built. What terrible secret of the Dwarves had I unlocked by building that thing? 

Lastly I noticed that there was a schematic below the one we'd been inspecting - and only then did I realise that the mirror I had already made was incomplete, and that my client was working from incomplete information. Aghast, we began our retreat to the city.

It was hours before we got out, and night had already fallen. We were able to get to the coastal path with the light of our torches, though, with various pieces of metal in tow. We headed to our homes, and resolved to meet the next day.

The following morning, we met in the square. We shook hands but said little, and went about the process of trying to work out who my client had actually been. Few people knew anything at all, and among those who did was a jeweller from whom she had commissioned a lens ground from a single huge piece of glass. She admitted she'd never seen the kind of glass used before, but for the sum she'd been paid, she'd been delighted to perform the work. We knew that this must be our woman - fortunately, she knew that our mutual client was Lady Mairyn of Anticlere, whose manor was not far away. 

We knocked on the door of the manor house, and were eventually received by an irate looking manservant who invited us in. He looked not to have slept recently - his eyes were red through with bloodshot.

"We haven't seen Lady Mairyn for a couple of days now," he said, "we last saw her when she went into her study to examine her new mirror". 
When I explained that I was the maker of the mirror and I was concerned it needed adjustments, he let us in.

Her study was very neat, and the bookshelves were crammed tight with many tomes, arcane and otherwise. I saw books on the nature of Orcs and of Snow Elves, and some in the language of the Nords and of the Dwarves, and holy texts of the Dunmer and the Alessian Order. And there on the wall hung the mirror, looking for all the world like an entry into another identical room. It was the same as I had last seen it, excepy for the glass lens that was set above it - I'd made space for it without knowing what it was for. I marvelled at it for a moment before my reverie was once again shattered by a yell from Razim.

The woman was in the mirror! She glared at me with purposeless rage, and lunged at me with a dagger. And by the Divines, it struck me! She was nowhere to be seen in the room, but in that mirror she attacked my own reflection with ferocity. I was able to dodge, and she lept for Razim - he blocked where the knife should have gone in with the shield, but the spectral knife went straight into his flesh. He howled in pain, and I tried to focus on what to do next. I grabbed the soul gem from my satchel, the one I'd taken from the golem, and did what the second schematic had shown me - I opened up the lens-hinge and tucked it in among the filaments behind the glass. Once ensconced, I pushed the lens shut, and-

Her reflection was gone. I staggered over to Razim, whose bleeding, like mine, had simply stopped. The pain ebbed for a moment until it was gone. We looked at each other in disbelief.

When the manservant appeared in the doorway a few moments later, stirred by our commotion, we had to simply assure him that everything was alright, and that our grumblings had been our surprise at the number of books we'd seen. It was a feeble excuse, thought of on the spur of the moment, but the man simply nodded serenely.

I never did find out what the mirror was used for. I have learned since then that the Dwarves, better known as the Dwemer, knew strange magics that could be channelled through metal thread without interruption. I learned that they could use sounds to shape things, and that they could field whole legions of the metal spheres we had encountered.

Two things still haunt me from that last encounter, though. One was the fact that the manservant seemed so much less haggard when he came to see us in the study than when he'd met us at the door. The other was the fact that when I last glanced in the mirror, I could have sworn that his reflection was the woman's.
