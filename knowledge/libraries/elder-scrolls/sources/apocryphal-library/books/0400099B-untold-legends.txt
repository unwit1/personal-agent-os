The Other Lives of Ysgramor

Even after the forgetting though, wisps of story find ways to receptive ears as even the deepest of secrets never truly dies. When fires burn and the night grows soft in the wilds of Eastmarch or Haafingar, there comes an old Nord shield-sister, clever-man or hunter who provides, in return for a horn of ale and enough food to sleep on, a kernel of an ancient story long forgotten - enough, by far, to keep an audience of Nords entertained in rapt silence until the fire dies low.

Many of these tales, in fact most of them, are on the matter of Ysgramor. The original tales may have been of different folk indeed, but it may be that their names were simply not remembered. Such is the currency of heroic fame.
[pagebreak]

<img src='img://Textures/Interface/Books/Illuminated_Letters/T_letter.png'>he Legend of Ysgramor and the Hound

To one-such group of warriors, a hunter did come and told this tale for a meal of venison and bread, a horn of jazbay-wine and the warmth of the embers.

All young Nords know the tales of Ysgramor and the Woodland Man, who appeared to the stripling diversely as a hare, a horker, a snowdrift, a scroll and many more things besides. Far fewer know the tale of the time the Woodland Man's crafty younger brother also tried to trick him. The brother did not appear to Atmorans so often as did Herma-Mora, for he was but a child, and his name is not well remembered, for some call him Klafyk and some called him Bjarbas. But among the gods, this brother is known as a trickster, who makes clever deals to the other's disadvantage. Ysgramor was used to such things by now from his meetings with Herma, but he had not heard of this brother before.

And so when Ysgramor, gathering lumber in the dangerous old high forest one day, heard a child crying - he wandered over to see. There, in a clearing, he did see the child - who looked like the child of men, but for the faun's antlers sprouting from his forehead. The child looked at him dolefully.

"Hail, Child. What are you doing out here? Be you man, or forest spirit?"

"Oh sir, I am as you say a spirit of the woods, but I am in great danger! A terrible hound is pursuing me! If you can fight it for me, I will reward you well!"

"I warn you child, if this is another trick of the Woodland Man..."

"Oh, he's my brother! He's boring though. He just likes to steal people's ideas and fates. Can you stop this hound for me? He's really dangerous!"

"Very well then child. I shall defeat this hound, and claim your reward."

And so did Ysgramor venture into the woods with his wood-axe by his side and Long-Launcher across his back, with a quiver of twelve stone arrows. Presently he came upon a clearing in which there paced a huge black dog. He drew his bow and loosed a shot at the hound, which was able to dodge. It came tearing towards him, and so he raised his axe. But he was crafty, and he knew something wasn't quite right. The dog did not leap for him at once, and wasn't growling or howling at all. 

He noticed that there was a chain around the dog's neck, and that the creature was wheezing somewhat as if choked. Thinking fast, he aimed long-launcher not at the dog itself, but at the top link of the chain. He severed the chain with a single stone arrow, and the hound gasped for air, able now to speak;

"My gratitude, wood-gatherer! No doubt my tricksy master bid you slay me in return for great reward, yes? He cannot vanquish me on his own, because he and I are as one, so he sometimes relies on the greed or kindness of others to get rid of me when I make him too bored. Know this; his reward will also be a trick, but if you are crafty you can make a very good deal.  Just don't lie to him, he can always smell an untruth."

Ysgramor returned through the woods, and covered his axe in the blood of a dead fox he passed by. He soon reached the clearing with the child.

"You're back! Is it done?"
"The hound shall hunger for you no more."
"It's dead then?"
He produced the axe with the blood on it, but said nothing.

Sensing no lie, the child said; 
"Wonderful! Then what will you choose - a drinking horn, a crown, or a sword? All are very powerful in their way."

"I would ask instead a different reward," said Ysgramor.
"Oh? By all means ask, and if it is in my power to grant it, perhaps I will," replied the child.
"Ah, then grant me the loyal hound you sent me to slay."

The child's face fell, as he saw his dog approaching, tail a-wagging.

"Bah, you have tricked me with a ruse, wood-gatherer, but you are the first among your kind ever to do so! For this, I shall grant you your wish, for I do enjoy being matched in cleverness and wit."

And so Ysgramor came to have at his call the first of many loyal hunting-dogs, and though the hound did not stay at his side forever, the victories they enjoyed together made Ysgramor famed indeed.

To another-such gathering of Hunters around a fire did there once come a Wise-Woman. She knew from the Owl-god Jhunal that wisdom is to be shared, and she knew from the Woodland Man that knowledge is to be sold. And so in exchange for her place at the fire, the company of her country-folk, a horn of mead and a wedge of cheese she told the following.

It is not widely known that there were many Dragons in Atmora. It is even said that so favoured of the dragons was Ysgramor, that he was given his name by them, and that others had been named this before.

When he returned to Atmora after the sacking of Saarthal, the dragons were glad to see him, but were sorrowful at his news.

"We did tell you that you would suffer in this new land, O Hoary-Bringer of Battle and Glory. The same thing happened when we went there, many ages ago."

"You went to the land as well? You never told me you had."

"It is so. Remember when we told you we named you after another? He went there, and something the same happened, and so he would return with all his vengeance on that land. But that was long ago, and much changed before we settled back here."

"Do I have your blessing to return?" replied Ysgramor, who had already begun to gather his companions.

"You do. But we will give to you a final gift." And so they laid a large war-drum before him, and it was large and had a great sound that he knew would carry well across the waves.

"This drum was made from the scales of your name-twin. Beat it well, and your ships will find their way back to the new land with ease. But beware your return also; for he suffered on his return as well, though great was his vengeance. Come, try out the drum."

And so Ysgramor did beat the large drum, and found that its sound was very deep and very old. So powerful was the noise that soon the heart-beat of every Atmoran matched its steady pace. The five-hundred gathered before him, unified in new purpose, as the instrument spoke its rhythmic doom of the warriors and those that they would face. The dragons spread their wings, for this sound was ancient even to them, and it was not the first time that they had commanded Ysgramor to play. 

To the beat of the drum, the remaining ships were assembled. To the beat of the drum, the weapons were forged. To the beat of the drum, the sails were hoisted. By this time, his beat had been in such perfect time that when he stopped beating it, the drum kept playing.

And it kept playing as it was mounted on the Ylgermet, and it kept playing as the Atmorans crossed the sea to the new land, with the dragons flying behind them. It did not stop playing until the first of the elves was slain in the new attack.

<img src='img://Textures/Interface/Books/Illuminated_Letters/T_letter.png'>he Legend of the Atmoran Civil War

A hooded man brought this tale into the ancient hall of Hroldan many years ago, and feasted alongside the Nords there in return. He spoke in a whisper that all could hear, and so it is believed he was a Greybeard.


It is known that Ysgramor was fleeing a civil war in Atmora when he first visited Skyrim, but the truth of the war is rarely remembered.

Atmora had been in a war long before that, when the armies of Shor had fought the armies of the Elf-Titan Trinimac. In those times, Orkey had granted great strength to Man, but at the cost of the long years that the elves enjoyed. It was said that some time later, Orkey - who was a snake, and Kyne - who was a hawk, did first give birth to dragons, who took on the aspect of both.

Thus was such proclaimed by several priests of the Dragon-Gods, who in turn drew the ire of the others. It cannot be, they said, for the dragons are clearly the children of Shor and of Alduin, who in those days looked for all the world like a Snake and an Eagle.

This brought a terrible rage in turn, for not only is Shor a fox and not a snake, but such a blasphemy that Alduin should be granted the same aspect of Kyne. Worse, it is said that the Eagle was how the Elf-Titans had painted Alduin, and it was not his true shape.
And so two factions of Dragon Priests went to war, with the dragons taking the side of either. At no point did the dragons ever issue their opinion on either story, or if they did it was not recorded.

It was the dragons, though, who resolved the conflict. It was decided that, for the war to end, neither case could be true, and that therefore dragons could only have been the children of a dragon-god, and it was decided that Alduin was he - though he was feared among the Atmorans because of his actions in the ancient Atmoran city of Lygge many ages past where he had slain Shor and his army of crabs. But the dragons were adamant - Alduin was the first of them, and he in turn was the son of a new-born dragon called AKA, whose voice was able to hold up the roof of the night sky.

It is claimed that Herma-Mora, the Demon of Knowledge, started the war by inspiring various priests of the truth of either side of the argument - that he was the enemy on both hands, a foe to all. But it is said that the demon howled with fear when he learned that Alduin was now king of the dragons, for he believed his knowledge would be taken by the Dragon forever.

And of Ysgramor, it is said that he would not take a side because he knew that both stories were false, and that he knew that all wars were fought over such falsehoods, and that he decided to live in a place of peace forever more. And when war came to Saarthal, it is said that the Night of Tears, for so it was named, was called such because of the weight of Ysgramor's tears alone, not just for his fallen comrades, but because he knew that war would always come - that one would always become hungry at the treasure of another.

But the least-spoken of all these is the legend, so far back now that the traces of its passage from myth to lore to campfire tale are like footprints after two-day snow, is the one that says; Ysgramor was a priest of the dragons, and he was the first to whom Herma-Mora spoke his lie. And it was the last trick that the Woodland Man played on the man, and it was the first that the man fell for.
