|Summary                                                                 |
|Excellence through diversity is one of the Company goals, yet the term  |
|diversity often raises controversy, confusion, and tension. What does it|
|mean? Is it the same as affirmative action? Why should you focus on it? |
|When people think of diversity, they may think first of ethnicity and   |
|race, and then gender; however, diversity is much broader than that.    |
|Diversity is otherness or those human qualities that are different from |
|our own and outside the groups to which we belong, yet present in other |
|individuals and groups. Dimensions of diversity include, but are not    |
|limited to: age, ethnicity, ancestry, gender, physical                  |
|abilities/qualities, race, sexual orientation, educational background,  |
|geographic location, income, marital status, military experience,       |
|religious beliefs, parental status, and work experience, according to   |
|Loden and Rosener in Workforce America! Managing Employee Diversity as a|
|Vital Resource.                                                         |
|It's important to understand how these dimensions affect performance,   |
|motivation, success, and interactions with others. Institutional        |
|structures and practices that have presented barriers to some dimensions|
|of diversity should be examined, challenged, and removed.               |
|Guiding Principles                                                      |
|Workforce diversity is a reality at RTEC.                               |
|Managing diversity is defined as "planning and implementing             |
|organizational systems and practices to manage people so that the       |
|potential advantages of diversity are maximized while its potential     |
|disadvantages are minimized," according to Taylor Cox in "Cultural      |
|Diversity in Organizations."                                            |
|Managing diversity well provides a distinct advantage in an era when    |
|flexibility and creativity are keys to competitiveness. An organization |
|needs to be flexible and adaptable to meet new customer needs.          |
|With effective management of diversity, RTEC develops a reputation as an|
|employer of choice. Not only will you have the ability to attract the   |
|best talent from a shrinking labor pool, you can save time and money in |
|recruitment and turnover costs.                                         |
|Managing Diversity                                                      |
|To address diversity issues, consider these questions: what policies,   |
|practices, and ways of thinking and within our organizational culture   |
|have differential impact on different groups? What organizational       |
|changes should be made to meet the needs of a diverse workforce as well |
|as to maximize the potential of all workers, so that RTEC can be well   |
|positioned for the demands of the 21st century?                         |
|Most people believe in the golden rule: treat others as you want to be  |
|treated. The implicit assumption is that how you want to be treated is  |
|how others want to be treated. But when you look at this proverb through|
|a diversity perspective, you begin to ask the question: what does       |
|respect look like; does it look the same for everyone? Does it mean     |
|saying hello in the morning, or leaving someone alone, or making eye    |
|contact when you speak?                                                 |
|It depends on the individual. We may share similar values, such as      |
|respect or need for recognition, but how we show those values through   |
|behavior may be different for different cultures. How do we know what   |
|different cultures need? Perhaps instead of using the golden rule, we   |
|could use the platinum rule which states: "treat others as they want to |
|be treated." Moving our frame of reference from an ethnocentric view    |
|("our way is the best way") to a culturally relative perspective ("let's|
|take the best of a variety of ways") will help us to manage more        |
|effectively in a diverse work environment.                              |
|Your Role                                                               |
|You have a key role in transforming the organizational culture so that  |
|it more closely reflects the values of our diverse workforce. Some of   |
|the skills needed are:                                                  |
|an understanding and acceptance of managing diversity concepts          |
|recognition that diversity is threaded through every aspect of          |
|management                                                              |
|self-awareness, in terms of understanding your own culture, identity,   |
|biases, prejudices, and stereotypes                                     |
|willingness to challenge and change institutional practices that present|
|barriers to different groups                                            |
|It's natural to want a cookbook approach to diversity issues so that one|
|knows exactly what to do. Unfortunately, given the many dimensions of   |
|diversity, there is no easy recipe to follow. Advice and strategies     |
|given for one situation may not work given the same situation in another|
|context.                                                                |
|Managing diversity means acknowledging people's differences and         |
|recognizing these differences as valuable; it enhances good management  |
|practices by preventing discrimination and promoting inclusiveness. Good|
|management alone will not necessarily help you work effectively with a  |
|diverse workforce. It is often difficult to see what part diversity     |
|plays in a specific area of management.                                 |
|To illustrate, the following two examples show how diversity is an      |
|integral part of management. The first example focuses on the area of   |
|selection, the second example looks at communication:                   |

|How can recruitment be effectively targeted to underutilized groups?    |
|How do you overcome cultural bias in the interviewing process,          |
|questions, and your response?                                           |

|Fair vs. Same Treatment                                                 |
|Many people think that "fairness" means "treating everyone the same."   |
|How well does treating everyone the same work for a diverse staff? For  |
|example, when employees have limited English language skills or reading |
|proficiency, even though that limit might not affect their ability to do|
|their jobs, transmitting important information through complicated memos|
|might not be an effective way of communicating with them. While         |
|distributing such memos to all staff is "treating everyone the same,"   |
|this approach may not communicate essential information to everyone. A  |
|staff member who missed out on essential information might feel that the|
|communication process was "unfair." A process that takes account of the |
|diverse levels of English language and reading proficiency among the    |
|staff might include taking extra time to be sure that information in an |
|important memorandum is understood. Such efforts on the part of         |
|supervisors and managers should be supported and rewarded as good       |
|management practices for working with a diverse staff.                  |
|Managing Diversity is Different from Affirmative Action                 |
|Managing diversity focuses on maximizing the ability of all employees to|
|contribute to organizational goals. Affirmative action focuses on       |
|specific groups because of historical discrimination, such as people of |
|color and women. Affirmative action emphasizes legal necessity and      |
|social responsibility; managing diversity emphasizes business necessity.|
|In short, while managing diversity is also concerned with under         |
|representation of women and people of color in the workforce, it is much|
|more inclusive and acknowledges that diversity must work for everyone.  |
|Consequences of Ignoring Diversity                                      |
|Ignoring diversity issues costs time, money, and efficiency. Some of the|
|consequences can include unhealthy tensions between people of differing |
|gender, race, ethnicity, age, abilities, etc.; loss of productivity     |
|because of increased conflict; inability to attract and retain talented |
|people of all kinds; complaints and legal actions; and inability to     |
|retain women and people of color, resulting in lost investments in      |
|recruitment and training.                                               |

|Do you test your assumptions before acting on them?                     |
|Do you believe there is only one right way of doing things, or that     |
|there are a number of valid ways that accomplish the same goal? Do you  |
|convey that to staff?                                                   |
|Do you have honest relationships with each staff member you supervise?  |
|Are you comfortable with each of them? Do you know what motivates them, |
|what their goals are, how they like to be recognized?                   |
|Are you able to give negative feedback to someone who is culturally     |
|different from you?                                                     |
|When you have open positions, do you insist on a diverse screening      |
|committee and make additional outreach efforts to ensure that a diverse |
|pool of candidates has applied?                                         |
|When you hire a new employee, do you not only explain job               |
|responsibilities and expectations clearly, but orient the person to RTEC|
|and department culture and unwritten rules?                             |
|Do you rigorously examine your unit's existing policies, practices, and |
|procedures to ensure that they do not differentially impact different   |
|groups? When they do, do you change them?                               |
|Are you willing to listen to constructive feedback from your staff about|
|ways to improve the work environment? Do you implement staff suggestions|
|and acknowledge their contribution?                                     |
|Do you take immediate action with people you supervise when they behave |
|in ways that show disrespect for others in the workplace, such as ethnic|
|jokes and offensive terms?                                              |
|Do you make good faith efforts to meet your affirmative action goals?   |
|Do you have a good understanding of institutional ism such as racism and|
|sexism and how they manifest themselves in the workplace?               |
|Do you ensure that assignments and opportunities for advancement are    |
|accessible to everyone?                                                 |

user's guide
Hiring and employing the right candidates is critical in today's fast-paced
workplace. The Amsterdam Printing Application for Employment  System  is  a
comprehensive, step-by-step guide that will help  you  through  the  hiring
process.

       part 1 enhanced job candidate interview system

Many "problem employees" are individuals who should have never  been  hired
in the first  place.  Human  resources  managers  must  ensure  that  their
personnel administration systems are utilized appropriately. This guide  is
designed to assist managers and supervisors  in  identifying  suitable  job
candidates and conducting appropriate pre-hire discussions with prospective
employees.

caveat:
Federal, state and local employment laws are intended to prevent employment
discrimination on the basis of race, gender, age and certain other  legally
protected status. Some of these considerations are reviewed in  Part  2  of
this User's Guide. It is imperative that all individuals  involved  in  the
recruitment  and  hiring  of  employees  be  familiar  with   the   legally
permissible techniques of hiring. Essentially, all inquiries and  purported
job qualifications must be clearly related to the job at issue. The general
rule: Judge the applicant on ability to perform the work, not  on  personal
characteristics.

3. the job interview
The purpose of the job interview is to gather and record relevant  opinions
of the interviewer regarding  the  job  candidate's  suitability  for  work
within  the  organization.  Although  the  interviewer  must  control   the
conversation, the job candidate should do most of the talking.

There are several areas of inquiry which can be  explored  as  a  means  of
securing key information about the job candidate that may  not  be  readily
apparent from a review of the application form and/or resume. Specifically,
it is worthwhile to further explore the following:



A. Inquire about the candidate's area of expertise and ask in detail  as  to
   the manner in which the candidate performs a given  task  by  asking  the
   candidate to explain, in detail, elements of his or her current position.
   The interviewer can gain a good deal of insight into  the  work  ethic  a
   candidate brings to the position as well as the value judgements used  by
   the candidate in determining  what  aspects  of  the  position  are  most
   important.
B. Ask the candidate why he or she is  leaving  his/her  current  job.  The
   candidate may not be happier with the new position.

C. Ask the candidate to identify  a  specific  emergency  that  had  to  be
   handled in a former job and ask for specific details concerning  how  the
   situation was managed. Responses to this type of question will  give  the
   interviewer a good idea of the job  candidate's  creativity  and  overall
   energy in approaching employment as well as the  candidate's  willingness
   to work overtime.
D. Ask the candidate to describe the work environment they are looking  for
   and what they would need from the hiring organization. Responses to  this
   type of question will indicate to the interviewer how well the  candidate
   would fit into the organization's work environment. Ask the candidate  to
   describe the best and worst type of person to work with. Answers to  this
   type of question should reveal the candidate's basic  sensitivity  toward
   others, to workplace issues and on-the-job cooperation. Ask the candidate
   the best and worst aspects of a former employment situation and  ask  for
   details. This also will indicate  to  the  interviewer  the  individual's
   ability to cooperate on the job.
E. If the interview is for a sales or other professional position,  be  sure
to ask the candidate if     there are  employment  contracts  or  any  other
legal restrictions that would impact his/her   ability  to  perform  on  the
job.

F. Be sure to ask the reasons for any gaps  in  the  applicant's  employment
history.
G. Ask the job candidate for a  self-assessment  of  his/her  strengths  and
weaknesses.
H. Ask the candidate what,  if  anything,  he  or  she  would  change  about
management at their last job.
I. Ask the candidate to share any questions he or  she  may  have  regarding
the position.
J. Ask the candidate  if  there  were  particular  areas  of  experience  or
qualifications that the     interviewer did not explore, that the  candidate
would like to discuss.





4. ending   the   interview

A. It is important that a starting and ending time is set at the beginning
of the interview (20-30 minutes is usually sufficient). It is helpful to
have a visible clock nearby so that both the interviewer and the candidate
knows when the interview will end.

    B. Let the candidate know what the next step in the hiring  process  is.
       This  includes  how  the  candidate  will  be  evaluated  within  the
       organization and how a decision regarding his or her  candidacy  will
       be communicated.

                 the  legal  framework  avoiding  inadvertent  employment
    discrimination

THE APPLICATION OF  DISCRIMINATION  LAWS  IN  THE  AMERICAN  WORKPLACE  HAS
UNDERGONE DRAMATIC CHANGE IN RECENT YEARS. EMPLOYERS,  OF  COURSE,  HAVE  A
RIGHT AND OBLIGATION TO ASK QUESTIONS OF PROSPECTIVE EMPLOYEES DESIGNED  TO
DETERMINE THE BEST  QUALIFIED  INDIVIDUALS  FOR  ALL  AVAILABLE  POSITIONS.
HOWEVER,  MANY  EMPLOYERS  FIND  THEMSELVES  DEFENDING  AGAINST  EMPLOYMENT
DISCRIMINATION CLAIMS BECAUSE THEY WERE NOT  AWARE  THAT  MANY  AVENUES  OF
INQUIRY ARE EITHER ILLEGAL  OR  SUBJECT  TO  DISCRIMINATORY  INTERPRETATION
UNDER VARIOUS STATE AND FEDERAL EQUAL EMPLOYMENT LAWS.

It is not even necessary for  an  employer  to  intentionally  discriminate
through an inquiry or to use improperly gathered information in  making  an
employment decision. Most anti-discrimination laws provide that the use  of
improper  questions  have  the  effect  of  discouraging  legally-protected
individuals from applying  for  positions  to  which  they  are  qualified.
Therefore, employers may face liability for  merely  bringing  up  subjects
considered impermissible.

As noted in Part 1 of  this  User's  Guide,  pre-employment  inquiries  and
established job qualifications must relate to the actual performance of the
work needed. Pre-hire inquiries should only seek  information  relative  to
the person's ability to perform  the  job  in  question,  with  or  without
reasonable accommodation as may be appropriate.
                           AVOIDING  TROUBLE SPOTS
Most employers properly pledge  not  to  discriminate  in  hiring  or  other
employment-related  decisions  on  race,  color,  religion,  creed,  gender,
national  origin,  age,  disability,  family  or  veteran   status,   sexual
orientation, or any other  legally-protected  status.  Direct  and  indirect
inquiries  into  such  areas  should  be  avoided.  The   Equal   Employment
Opportunity  laws  (EEO)  of  the  Federal   government   and   most   state
jurisdictions  highlight  various  "trouble  spots"  which  all  individuals
involved in the recruitment and hiring of employees  should  be  trained  to
avoid. The following is a general review of such topics.

age of applicant
Federal law prohibits discrimination on the basis of age for all applicants
between the ages of 18 and  65;  therefore,  avoid  any  employment-related
inquiry as to the age of an applicant. The hiring organization should avoid
any direct or indirect inquiry into the age of an applicant, even where the
employer may believe he or she has a legitimate,  nondiscriminatory  reason
for obtaining the information.


MARITAL AND FAMILY STATUS
It is improper for an employer to inquire into the marital  status  of  any
prospective employee. Perceived family responsibilities, noting whether the
job represents a "first" or "second" income, and seeking information as  to
an applicant's possible "future plans to become a parent", are all examples
of impermissible pre-employment inquiries. Similarly,  an  employer  should
not inquire into the family composition of an applicant or  into  the  work
arrangements of an applicant's spouse.
An employer should not generally inquire  into  a  job  candidate's  arrest
record, except where a  conviction  is  involved  (the  person  was  proven
guilty). A past arrest that did not lead to  a  conviction  should  not  be
given employment-related consideration (the  person  in  question  was  not
proven guilty). Where conviction is involved, or an arrest is pending  that
is related to the job at issue, an employer may choose  to  either  suspend
the employment decision until the arrest matter is resolved, or advise  the
job applicant to re-submit his or her application after resolution  of  the
matter. An arrest should never be  the  basis  of  a  hiring  or  discharge
decision.

availability  for  saturday or sunday  work
In many situations, a general inquiry as to whether or not an applicant  is
available for Saturday or Sunday work may violate Federal and  state  laws,
since the question may discourage applicants  of  certain  religions  which
prohibit such work. Discrimination laws  on  this  subject  often  prohibit
discrimination  on  the  basis   of   religion   and   require   reasonable
accommodation of a person's religious  beliefs  and  practices.  Where  the
hiring organization can demonstrate that  it  needs  to  inquire  into  the
availability for Saturday or Sunday work due  to  its  legitimate  business
interests, it may ask the question but should also indicate  that  it  will
make a reasonable effort to accommodate the religious beliefs and practices
of its employees.


citizernship  and national origin  questions
Federal and most state laws prohibit employment discrimination on the basis
of national origin. Employers should inquire as to whether a job  applicant
may lawfully become employed in the United States, but should  not  inquire
into the specific citizenry of the applicant.










 Credit  record and financial status
Many employment discrimination laws are based on a premise,  in  part,  hat
consideration of the credit record or financial status of an applicant will
tend to have an adverse  impact  on  minorities.  Unless  an  employer  can
clearly show that it needs to inquire into this area due to its  legitimate
business interests, inquiries into the financial  status  of  an  applicant
should be avoided.

family relationship to other employees
Inquiries into the family relationship a prospective employee  may  have  to
current employees should be avoided. Such questions  may  reflect  a  hiring
preference for  friends  or  relatives  of  employees  which  works  to  the
exclusion of women or minorities. While many  employers  have  worked  under
rules which prohibit spouses from working for the  same  organization,  such
rules are currently under very strict scrutiny since there is an  increasing
belief that the rule does not serve a clear business  purpose.  However,  it
appears that a policy whereby one spouse is prohibited  from  working  under
the direct supervision of the other is permissible.

disability  status
The Americans with Disabilities Act of 1990 has  more  clearly  defined  an
employer's  obligations  in  the   workplace   regarding   disability-based
employment discrimination. Federal and most state employment  laws  protect
qualified disabled individuals with physical  or  mental  impairments,  and
also protects individuals who are perceived to have such  disabilities.  If
an employer inquires about  the  physical  or  mental  capabilities  of  an
individual to perform a particular job, the inquiry  should  only  be  made
after the potential employee  has  been  specifically  informed  about  the
requirements of the job in question, and after the hiring organization  has
indicated that it can reasonably accommodate employees with disabilities.
wage  garnishment  record
Employers should not, as a rule, inquire as to the  garnishment  record  of
any job applicant.

height, weight, eye  and  hair  color questions
Height and weight questions  should  be  avoided  since  they  are  not  job
related and have been  found  to  exclude  members  of  certain  nationality
groups as well as individuals considered to  be  protected  by  Federal  and
state disability discrimination laws. The same holds true for eye  and  hair
color questions.

gender-related questions
It is not permissible for an employer to base hiring  or  other  employment-
related  decisions  on  the  sex  of  an  applicant.  (There  are  very  few
circumstances where gender may be a "bone  fide  occupational  qualification
reasonably necessary to the normal  operation"  of  a  business.)  Employers
should be forewarned that there are many indirect inquiries which run  afoul
of gender discrimination rules as well as other anti-discrimination rules.

Trouble spots in this regard include inquiries into an  applicant's  maiden
or prior name, whether an applicant wishes to  be  called  "Mr.,  Miss,  or
Mrs." and the applicant's spouse's name.
'honesty"  testing
Employers should not base hiring  or  other  employment  decisions  on  the
results of a polygraph or similar physiologically-based test. In  addition,
written tests are closely scrutinized, since many have been found  to  tend
to exclude or preclude the hiring of women and minorities.

questions  regarding  educational  background
Many employers believe that there is no potential legal  liability  related
to inquiries regarding the educational background of prospective employees.
However, consideration of  formal  education  in  making  hiring  decisions
related to jobs where such education is  not  job  related  may,  in  fact,
violate discrimination laws  where  disparate  impact  on  minority  groups
results. Employers should be able to show that all  areas  of  inquiry  are
related to job performance, even where the area of  inquiry  is  considered
"safe", such as educational background.
