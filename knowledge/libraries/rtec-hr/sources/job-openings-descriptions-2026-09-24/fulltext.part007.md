Policy Statement

Purpose
The purpose of this exposure control plan is to eliminate or minimize employee occupational
exposure to blood or other potentially infectious materials as detailed in the Bloodborne
Pathogens standard.

Scope
This policy applies to all employees whose normal duties can be reasonably expected to place
them in contact with bloodborne pathogens or other potentially infectious materials.

Policy
It is the policy of this Rural Transit Enterprises Coordinated (RTEC) to:

Ensure all employees at risk are trained and provided an opportunity to receive immunization
required by state and federal regulation.

RTEC will regard all medical information as strictly confidential. Employee medical records will
be released only to the subject employee, to anyone having written authorization from the
subject employee, to the Director of the National Institute for Occupational Safety and Health,
U.S. Department of Health and Human Services or designated representative, the Assistant
Secretary of Labor for Occupational Safety and Health, or designated representative, and as is
required by law in accordance with 29 CFR 1910.20 as amended.

Objectives
        To prevent exposure to bloodborne pathogens or other potentially infectious materials.
        To provide appropriate education and training to employees who may be exposed to
         bloodborne pathogens or other potentially infectious materials while performing their
         job.
        To provide treatment and counseling to employees should exposure to bloodborne
         pathogens occur.

Definitions
Blood – means human blood, human blood components, and products made from human blood.

Bloodborne Pathogens – means pathogenic microorganisms that are present in human blood
and can cause disease in humans. These pathogens include, but are not limited to, Hepatitis B
virus (HBV) and human immunodeficiency virus (HIV).

Contaminated – means the presence or the reasonably anticipated presence of blood or other
potentially infectious materials on an item or surface.

Decontamination – means the use of physical or chemical means to remove, inactivate, or
destroy bloodborne pathogens on a surface or item to the point where they are no longer capable
of transmitting infectious particles and the surface or item is rendered safe for handling, use, or
disposal.
                                                                                                 3

Exposure Incident – means a specific eye, mouth, other mucous membrane, non-intact skin, or
parenteral contact with blood or other potentially infectious materials that results from the
performance of an employee’s duties.

Hand-washing Facilities – means a facility providing an adequate supply of running potable
water, soap and single use towels or air drying machines.

HBV – means hepatitis B virus.

HIV – means human immunodeficiency virus.

Licensed Healthcare Professional – is a person whose legally permitted scope of practice allows
him or her to independently perform the activities medically required for evaluation and
treatment of exposure and post-exposure Evaluation and Follow-up. (Example: can provide
Hepatitis B Vaccination)

Occupational Exposure – means reasonably anticipated skin, eye, mucous membrane, or
parental contact with blood or other potentially infectious materials that may result from the
performance of an employee’s duties.

Other Potentially Infectious Materials (OPIM) – means the following:
       1. Body fluids: including semen, vaginal secretions, cerebrospinal fluid, synovial fluid,
          pleural fluid, pericardial fluid, peritoneal fluid, amniotic fluid, saliva in dental
          procedures, any body fluid that is visibly contaminated with blood, and all body fluids
          in situations where it is difficult or impossible to differentiate between body fluids.
       2. Any unfixed tissue or organ (other than intact skin) from a human (living or dead).
       3. HIV containing cell or tissue cultures, organ cultures, and HIV or HBV containing
          culture medium or other solutions, and blood, organs, or other tissues from
          experimental animals infected with HIV or HBV.
       4. Known or highly suspected infectious airborne TB.

Parenteral – means piercing mucous membranes or the skin barrier through such events as
needle-sticks, human bites, cuts, and abrasions.

Personal Protective Equipment (PPE) – is specialized clothing or equipment worn/used by an
employee for protection against a hazard. General work clothes (e.g. uniforms, pants, shirts, or
blouses) that are not intended to function as protection against a hazard are not considered to
be personal protective equipment.

Purified Protein Derivative (PPD) Skin Testing – Injection of PPD just beneath the surface of the
forearm.

Regulated Waste – The OSHA Bloodborne Pathogens Standard directly defines “regulated waste”
to be:
      liquid or semi-liquid blood or other potentially infectious materials (OPIM);
      items contaminated with blood or OPIM and which would release these substances in a
       liquid or semi-liquid state if compressed;
                                                                                          4

      items that are caked with dried blood or OPIM and are capable of releasing these
       materials during handling;
      contaminated sharps; and
      pathological and microbiological wastes containing blood or OPIM

Refer to OSHA & CDC and local regulation guidance on regulated waste.

Source Individual – means any individual, living or dead, whose blood or other potentially
infectious materials may be a source of occupational exposure to the employee.

Sterilize – means the use of a physical or chemical procedure to destroy all microbial life including
highly resistant bacterial endospores.

Tuberculosis (TB) – is a contagious airborne lung disease.

Universal Precautions – is an approach to infection control. According to the concept of
Universal Precautions, all human blood and bodily fluids are treated as if known to be infectious
for HIV, HBV, and other bloodborne pathogens.

Work Practice Controls – means controls that reduce the likelihood of exposure by altering the
manner in which a task is performed.

Exposure Determination
RTEC recognizes that some of its employees may be exposed to blood or other infectious
materials. Wearing appropriate PPE can significantly reduce risk and exposure to Bloodborne
Pathogens, since it acts as a barrier against exposure. RTEC provides PPE to employees.

Job classifications at RTEC that may incur occupational exposure are: Drivers (Full and Part-Time),
Dispatcher, Custodial/Janitorial (including positions that require custodial/janitorial
assignments), and any other employees trained to render first aid in an emergency situation.
Refer to RTEC’s Transit Agency Safety Plan and the Job Hazard Analysis by Job Title/Category for
more information.

Implementation Schedule and Methodology
OSHA also requires that this plan include a schedule and method of implementation for the
various requirements of the standard. The following complies with this requirement:

Compliance Methods
       1. Universal Precautions will be observed at this facility in order to prevent contact with
          blood or other potentially infectious materials. All blood or other potentially
          infectious material will be considered infectious regardless of the perceived status of
          the source individual.
       2. RTEC shall provide hand-washing facilities which are readily accessible to employees.
          The public restrooms are located in the back of the RTEC Lobby building, in the front

                                                                                                   5

           of the RTEC Garage. Each Department also has restrooms available to staff. The
           Laurel County and Pulaski County facilities are also equipped with restrooms.

           Hands shall be washed with soap and running water as soon as feasible after removal
           of gloves or other personal protective equipment. If soap or running water is not
           immediately available, an alcohol-based hand rub or sanitizer should be used until
           soap and water are available.

           RTEC will provide either an appropriate hand cleaner with clean paper towels or
           drying towels, or antiseptic towelettes for each vehicle.

           RTEC will provide hot and cold water and paper towels or air drying mechanisms in
           each restroom at RTEC facilities. Employees shall wash hands and any other skin
           exposed with soap water, or flush mucous membranes with water when occupational
           exposure occurs or is thought to have occurred. RTEC also provides an eye wash
           station and shower located at the RTEC Garage.

           Hand Sanitizer and PPE as needed are also provided and readily available throughout
           RTEC facilities in various accessible locations.

Work Practices
All procedures will be conducted in a manner that will minimize splashing, spraying, splattering,
and generation of droplets of blood or other potentially infectious materials.

       1. An absorbent/sterilizing material will be used on large amounts of blood or other
          potentially infectious materials that have collected.
       2. High-pressure water spraying or aerosol spraying should be avoided if at all possible
          in clean up of blood or other potentially infectious materials.
       3. To minimize the handling of infectious waste only those trained in Bloodborne
          Pathogens and/or First Aid Responders will clean-up, decontaminate, and dispose of
          waste.

Personal Protective Equipment (PPE)
All personal protective equipment used at this facility will be provided without cost to employees.
Personal Protective equipment will be chosen based on the anticipated exposure to blood or
other potentially infectious materials. The protective equipment will be considered appropriate
only if it does not permit blood or other potentially infectious materials to pass through or reach
the employees clothing, skin, eyes, mouth or other mucous membranes under normal conditions
of use and for the duration of time which the protective equipment will be used.

Personal Protective Equipment will be provided to employees in the RTEC facility and in each
vehicle in the following manner:

   Disposable Protective Gloves will be accessible in the RTEC facilities and on each RTEC vehicle
    in the event an employee would need to render First Aid or clean-up a body fluid spill.
    Hypoallergenic gloves will be provided to employees who require them. Gloves (at a

     minimum) shall be worn whenever it is anticipated that employees will have hand contact
     with blood, other potentially infectious materials, non-intact skin, and mucous membranes.

    Disposable respirator masks will be accessible in the RTEC facilities and on each RTEC vehicle
     in the event an employee would be exposed to known or highly suspected TB or other
     airborne pathogen.

    All garments that are penetrated by blood shall be removed immediately or as soon as

Housekeeping
Decontamination of areas in the RTEC vehicles, in the RTEC offices, or at the RTEC garage which
have been contaminated with blood or other potentially infectious materials will be
accomplished by utilizing approved sanitizing materials, the spill clean-up kit and universal
precautions. Absorbent material and toweling should be disposed of in an appropriate manner
and in an appropriate container or bag. Red biohazard labeled bags should be used for known
infectious fluids and materials. These include blood, other potentially infectious material, and
body fluids which are not normally infectious, but which contain visible blood. OSHA does not
                                                                                               7

classify urine, feces or vomit as “a potentially infectious material.” Universal precautions may
not apply to feces, saliva (other than saliva from dental procedures), nasal secretions, sputum,
sweat, tears, urine and vomit unless they contain visible blood or are known to contain infectious
material. However, the clean up should include gloves and any other appropriate PPE as a
general precaution. The general guideline should be, “if it’s a bodily fluid and it’s not yours, don’t
touch it without protecting yourself with minimum PPE such as gloves.” If it is difficult or
impossible to differentiate between body fluid types in a particular circumstance, all body fluids
must be considered potentially infectious material.

The existence of regulated waste determination is not to be based on actual volume of blood,
rather on the potential to release blood or OPIM, e.g., when compacted in the waste container.
OSHA does not generally consider discarded feminine hygiene products, used to absorb
menstrual flow, to fall within the definition of regulated waste. OSHA expects the waste
containers into which these products are discarded to be lined in such a way as to protect
employees from physical contact with the contents. Bandages which are not saturated to the
point of releasing blood or OPIM if compressed would not be considered as regulated waste.

Housekeeping should also remain consistent with recommendations from OSHA and the CDC
regarding current Norovirus and Coronavirus (COVID19) guidelines. All general housekeeping is
with fresh chlorine bleach solutions or EPA germicides or equivalent. EPA-registered
disinfectants should be used according to manufacturers’ instructions, including the use of
proper PPE recommended by the manufacturer when applying the product.

Hepatitis B Vaccine
       1. All employees who have been identified as having potential exposure to blood or
          other potentially infectious materials will be offered the Hepatitis B vaccine within 24
          hours of exposure unless the employee has previously had the vaccine.
       2. The Hepatitis B vaccine will be offered to the employee as a First Aid responder within
          24 hours of exposure unless the employee has previously had the vaccine.
       3. Employees who initially decline the Hepatitis B vaccine will sign a waiver stating that
          they decline the vaccination but realize that in the future if they want the vaccination
          after an exposure incident, they can receive it at no charge to them.
       4. Currently both the Hepatitis B and the Hepatitis A Vaccine is available to employees
          utilizing RTEC health care benefits insurance at no cost. Employees should contact
          their health care professional to discuss vaccination options. Employees are
          encouraged to research vaccine ingredients and manufacturing process when making
          the determination.

Purified Protein Derivative (PPD) Skin Testing
       1. All employees who have been identified as having potential exposure to known or
          highly suspected Tuberculosis (TB) will be offered the PPD Skin Test, at no cost to the
          employee. (No longer required annually of Safety-Sensitive Drivers as of 4/13/2012)
       2. The skin test will be offered to the employee unless the employee has previously had
          the test.
       3. Employees who initially decline the PPD skin test will sign a waiver stating that they
          decline the test but realize that in the future if they want the test, they can receive it
          at no charge to them.

       4. If Evaluation of employee is necessitated at their local Health Department, a PPD Skin
          Test may be recommend at that time. The Health Dept. may advise that a tuberculin
          skin test is not indicated at this time for various reasons. If this is the case,
          documentation from the Health Dept. is still required for your employee file (e.g. an
          Evaluation Form) for work related incidents.
       5. The employee should not have more than one TB Skin Test within the same year
          unless otherwise recommended by the Health Dept. or a Health Care professional.
       6. If they recommend a Chest X-ray, these are only required once every 5 years unless

       7. RTEC will adhere to Health Department recommendations regarding PPD Skin Tests

       8. In order to be reimbursed for a job related PPD Skin Test requirement, employees
          must return a receipt with the PPD form to RTEC. Most local Health Departments are
          willing to bill RTEC directly.

Evaluation of Circumstances Surrounding Exposure Incidents
Occupational exposure is defined by OSHA as: "reasonably un-anticipated skin, eye, mucous
membrane, non-intact skin, or parenteral contact with blood and other potentially infectious
materials that may result from the performance of an employee's duties." When the employee
incurs an exposure incident, complete immediate basic first aid if possible: (a.) Wounds and skin
should be washed with soap and water (avoid “milking” or squeezing needlestick injuries or wounds);
(b.) Flush exposed mucous membranes with water; (c.) Flush exposed eyes with water or saline
solution; (d.) Do NOT apply caustic agents, or inject antiseptics or disinfectants into the wound;
(e.) Seek professional medical attention as soon as possible.

When the employee incurs an exposure incident, it should be reported as soon as feasible to
their immediate supervisor, Human Resources (HR), Shirley L. Cummins (Executive Director), or
to Dale Shelton (Coordinator & Designated First Responder) at 1-800-321-7832 or 606-256-9835.
All employees who incur an exposure incident will be offered post-exposure evaluation and
follow-up. This follow-up may include the following:

       1. Documentation of the route of exposure and the circumstances related to the
          incident.
       2. If possible, the identification of the source individual and, if possible, the status of the
          source individual. The blood of the source individual will be tested (after consent is
          obtained) for HIV/HBV infectivity. RTEC understands that current confidentiality and
          healthcare regulations may limit access to certain information.
       3. Results of testing of the source individual will be made available to the exposed
          employee with the exposed employee informed about the applicable laws and
          regulations concerning disclosure of the identity and infectivity of the source
          individual.
       4. The employee will be offered the option of having their blood collected for testing of
          the employee’s HIV/HBV serological status
       5. The employee will be offered post exposure prophylaxis in accordance with the
          current recommendations and treatment options of the U.S. Public Health Service.
       6. The employee will be given appropriate counseling (by qualified individual)
          concerning precautions to take during the period after the exposure incident. The

                                                                                                    9

          employee will also be given information on what potential illnesses to be alert for and
          to report any related experience to appropriate personnel.
       7. First Aid providers who have not received prior immunizations will be offered the full
          immunization series with 24 hours of the exposure incident.
       8. The following persons(s) will maintain this policy: Dale Shelton (Coordinator &
          Designated First Responder); worker’s compensation/injury records will be
          maintained by the RTEC Human Resource Department.

Interaction with Health Care Professionals
Certain information is required to be provided to the health care professional(s) responsible for
providing evaluations, tests, and/or vaccinations. Such information may include:

       1. A copy of this policy;
       2. A description of the exposed employee’s duties as they relate to the exposure incident;
       3. Documentation of the routes of the exposure and circumstances under which exposure
          occurred;
       4. Results of the source individuals blood testing;
       5. All medical records relevant to the appropriate treatment of the employee.

A written opinion may be requested from the health care professional evaluating employees of
this facility. Written opinions will be obtained in the following instances:

   Whenever the employee is sent to obtain the Hepatitis B vaccine.
   Whenever the employee is sent to a health care professional following an exposure incident.

Health Care Professionals should limit their opinions to:
       1. Whether the Hepatitis B vaccine is needed at this time and if the employee has received the
          vaccine, or for evaluation following an incident.
       2. Any other recommended treatment.
       3. Informing the employee of the results of the evaluation, and
       4. Informing the employee about any medical conditions resulting from exposure to blood or
          other potentially infectious materials.

The Health Care Professional will discuss any other related services recommended as a result of
exposure with RTEC prior to said services taking place. Treatment and follow-up will be based
on the agent and type of exposure.

Training
Educational training on bloodborne pathogens will be provided for all employees who may incur
occupational exposure. Refresher training will be provided to employees at risk annually and as
often as needed. Refresher training will also be provided when the employee is scheduled for
CPR and/or First Aid re-certification. Employees will also receive training whenever there are
changes that affect the employee’s occupational exposure. The training for RTEC employees will
include the following:

       1. An accessible copy of the policy;
       2. A general explanation of the epidemiology and symptoms of bloodborne diseases;
       3. An explanation of the modes of transmission of bloodborne pathogens;

                                                                                                  10

       4. An explanation of the appropriate methods for recognizing tasks and other activities that may
           involve exposure to blood and other potentially infectious materials;
       5. An explanation of the use and limitations of methods that will prevent or reduce exposure
           including appropriate engineering controls, work practices, and personal protective
           equipment;
       6. Information on the types, proper use, location, removal, handling, decontamination and
           disposal of personal protective equipment;
       7. An explanation of the basis for selection of personal protective equipment;
       8. Information on the Hepatitis B vaccine, including information on its efficacy, safety, method
           of administration, the benefits of being vaccinated, and that the vaccine and vaccination will
           be offered free of charge if employee is exposed and the vaccine is recommended by a health
           care professional;
       9. Information on the TB evaluation and PPD skin test;
       10. Information on the appropriate actions to take and persons to contact in an emergency
           involving blood or other potentially infectious materials;
       11. Incident Procedures if an exposure occurs, including the method of reporting and the medical
           follow up that will be made available.
       12. An explanation on the signs and labels and/or color coding required by OSHA standards ( This
           portion may be completed in Hazard Communications and/or BBP Training).
       13. An opportunity for interactive questions and answers with the person conducting the training
           session.

The “National Transit Institute Infectious Disease Pocket Guide for Transit Employees” is a
valuable educational resource and RTEC accepts it as an addendum to this existing policy. It is
located as Attachment VI with this policy and may be used as a training resource. Training videos,
powerpoint presentations, lecture, seminars, reading materials (including this policy) may be
utilized to complete training. Training and training materials are available by contacting the
Training Coordinator or Human Resources. See Attachment IV & V of this policy for Training Quiz
and Answers on bloodborne pathogens.

Record Keeping
Medical Records
RTEC shall establish and maintain an accurate record for each employee with occupational
exposure. Medical records are maintained by the Human Resource Department. These records
shall include:

       1. The name and social security number of the employee and contact information.
       2. A copy of the employee’s Hepatitis B vaccination status including the dates of all the Hepatitis
          vaccinations and any medical records relative to the employee’s ability to receive vaccination.
       3. A copy of all results of examinations, medical testing, and follow up procedures.
       4. RTEC’s copy of the Healthcare Professional’s written opinion and recommendations and any
          other information provided.

Confidentiality
RTEC shall ensure that employee medical records are:

       1. Kept confidential.
       2. Not disclosed or reported without the employee’s express written consent to any person
          within or outside the workplace except as required by 1910.1030 or as may be required by
          law.
                                                                                                       11

       3. Maintained for the duration of the employment plus 30 years in accordance with 29 CFR
          1010.20.

Training Records
Training records shall be maintained for at least three (3) years from the date on which the
training occurred. Records should include: the dates of the training session(s); a summary of the
training session; the names and qualifications of the instructor(s) conducting the training; and
the names and job titles of all trainees.

Availability
RTEC shall ensure that all records required to be maintained shall be made available upon request
to duly authorized person(s). An employee’s training records shall be provided upon request for
examination and copying to the subject employee, to anyone having express written consent of
the subject employee, to the Director of the National Institute for Occupational Safety and
Health, U.S. Department of Health and Human Services or designated representative, the
Assistant Secretary of Labor for Occupational Safety and Health, or designated representative,
and as is required by law in accordance with 29 CFR 1910.20 as amended.

Transfer of Records
If RTEC ceases to do business and there is no successor employer to receive and retain these
records for the prescribed period, RTEC shall notify the Director of the National Institute for
Occupational Safety and Health, U.S. Department of Health and Human Services or designated
representative at least three (3) months prior to their disposal and transmit them to the Director,
if required to do so within that three (3) month period.

Maintenance of Records
All related records will be maintained by RTEC’s Coordinator and/or Human Resources
Department as required. Any exposure incident will be evaluated to determine if the case meets
OSHA’s Recordkeeping Requirements.

Contagion Preparedness
RTEC realizes that operations may be affected in an instance of severe influenza outbreak or
similar contagion like H1N1 Swine Flu or COVID-19. RTEC will work closely with local health
departments and government officials and representatives from the Center for Disease Control
(CDC) adhering to any recommendations and guidance. During the initial stages of the COVID-19
outbreak, and at the advice of the CDC and Transit Industry Leaders, RTEC further developed a
Contagious Virus Response Plan (CVRP) in March of 2020 as an addendum to the RTEC Transit
Agency Safety Plan. The plan is updated annually. Refer to RTEC’s Contagious Virus Response
Plan (CVRP) Plan and the RTEC Transit Agency Safety Plan for information on this subject.

RTEC will strive to keep any service interruption limited. Influenza is thought to spread mainly
person-to-person through coughing or sneezing of infected people, so advising employees to
follow public health recommendations is crucial to prevention of spreading the disease. RTEC
will educate its employees by posting educational materials and sending out fliers and messages
regarding information and prevention methods recommended by local health officials and the
CDC.

How to Wear Cloth Face Coverings
Cloth face coverings should—
• fit snugly but comfortably against the side of the face
• be secured with ties or ear loops
• include multiple layers of fabric
• allow for breathing without restriction
• be able to be laundered and machine dried without damage or
  change to shape

CDC on Homemade Cloth Face Coverings
CDC recommends wearing cloth face coverings in public settings where other
social distancing measures are difficult to maintain (e.g., grocery stores and
pharmacies), especially in areas of significant community-based transmission.
CDC also advises the use of simple cloth face coverings to slow the spread of
the virus and help people who may have the virus and do not know it from
transmitting it to others. Cloth face coverings fashioned from household
items or made at home from common materials at low cost can be used as an
additional, voluntary public health measure.
Cloth face coverings should not be placed on young children under age
2, anyone who has trouble breathing, or is unconscious, incapacitated or
otherwise unable to remove the cloth face covering without assistance.
The cloth face coverings recommended are not surgical masks or N-95
respirators. Those are critical supplies that must continue to be reserved for
healthcare workers and other medical first responders, as recommended by
current CDC guidance.

Should cloth face coverings be washed or otherwise
cleaned regularly? How regularly?
Yes. They should be routinely washed depending on the frequency
of use.

How does one safely sterilize/clean a cloth face covering?
A washing machine should suffice in properly washing a cloth face covering.

How does one safely remove a used cloth face covering?
Individuals should be careful not to touch their eyes, nose, and mouth
when removing their cloth face covering and wash hands immediately
after removing.

    Drug & Alcohol Program: Frequently Asked Questions
Questions (Q) & Answers (A):
Q: Can You Really Fool a Breath Alcohol Test by Putting a Penny in Your Mouth?

A: The truth is, sucking on a penny, or any coin of any denomination for that matter, has absolutely no effect on a breath
test machine. You could put a hundred pennies in your mouth and it won't make a bit of difference, except maybe to your
oral hygiene! And the risk of choking to death.

Q: Can Mouthwash interfere with a Breath Alcohol Test?

A: No, if it is done correctly. According to the DOT regulations, one must perform a confirmation test and wait 15
minutes to allow for the residual mouth alcohol to clear. The breath alcohol test measures deep lung air and the residual
mouth alcohol is gone in as little as 5 minutes, so that 15 minutes is plenty of time to wait and do the confirmation test.
Any positive by saliva must be confirmed by a breath alcohol device.

Q: What about Poppy Seeds? Will they cause a positive test for Opiates?

A: Heroin comes from poppies. Poppies come from Poppy seeds. The truth is that there is a small amount of morphine
in a poppy seed. In December 1998, the DOT raised the cutoff to 2000 ng/mL to eliminate poppy seeds from causing a
problem. The Medical Review Officer reviews laboratory test results to see if there is another possible explanation for a
positive laboratory result.

Q: How does Marijuana Affect Driving?

A: Marijuana has adverse effects on many of the skills needed for driving a vehicle. These may include difficulty in
judging distances and delayed reactions to sights and sounds. Smoking marijuana seems to affect eht structure of the
brain associated with emotions, motivation, and the regulation of hormones. THC also disrupts the nerve cells in the part
of the brain where memories are formed. Some studies show that when people have smoked large amounts of marijuana
for many years, the drug takes its toll on mental functions.

There are data showing that marijuana plays a role in crashes. A study of patients in a shock-trauma unit who had been in
traffic accidents revealed that 15 percent of those who had been driving a car or motorcycle had been smoking marijuana,
and another 17 percent had both THC and alcohol in their blood. In Memphis, Tennessee, researches found that of 150
reckless drivers who were tested for drugs at the arrest scene, 33 percent showed signs of marijuana use. It is also
important to note that when users combine marijuana with other drugs, such as alcohol, as they often do, the hazards of
driving can be more severe than with either drug alone.

Driving experiments show that marijuana affects a wide range of skills needed for safe driving. Thinking and reflexes are
slowed, making it hard for drivers to respond to sudden, unexpected events. Furthermore a driver’s ability to “track”
through curves, rake quickly and maintain proper speed and distance between vehicles is affected. Research shows that
these skills are impaired up to 24 hours AFTER smoking a single marijuana cigarette. People who smoke marijuana are
often never aware of how their actions affect the safety of others around them. Distorted space and time translates into
unsafe work habits. For more about the TRUTH about Drugs, visit www.drugfreeworld.org .

Q: How long is Marijuana detectable on a Urine Test?

A: THC in marijuana is strongly absorbed by fatty tissues in various organs. Generally, traces (metabolites) of THC can be
detected by standard urine testing methods several days after a smoking session. However, in heavy chronic users, traces
can sometimes be detected for weeks after they have stopped using marijuana.

Q: I don’t have a drug problem. Why should I be tested?

A: The purpose of the drug testing is to protect your safety and the public safety. If you are a DOT covered employee,
tested is authorized under 49 CFR Part 40 as required under the Omnibus Transportation Testing Act of 1991. RTEC
conducts Pre-employment, Random, Post-Accident and Reasonable Suspicion testing as required by FTA.

Stay alert and safe during deer
    season

    September 30, 2021

    By Steve Clary, KACo Loss Control Specialist

    October marks the beginning of the fall season, and with it comes an
    increase in auto collisions involving deer.
    The number of deer collisions dramatically increases during the last quarter
    of the year, which coincides with the rut and the majority of the deer
    hunting season in Kentucky. The majority of collisions occur after dark, so
    drivers should be more cautious while driving at night.

    The Safety and Loss Control Department offers the following tips for
    avoiding deer collisions:

   Stay alert. Observe signs (deer crossing) posted by the state highway
    department, which has identified areas that are known for wildlife crossing
    the road.
   Turn on high beams while driving with headlights.
   Don’t swerve if a crash is inevitable, and don’t veer off the road.
   Slow down. Brake as necessary and only brake hard if someone is closely
    following you. Tap your brakes to warn other drivers behind you.

    Reminders

   Peak season is October through December.
   Peak time for deer collisions is between dusk and dawn.
   If you see one deer, there are probably more nearby.

    Helpful hint: Most deer collisions are covered by comprehensive coverage
    with most insurers and is usually very affordable relative to the amount of
    coverage.

Defensive Driving Tips to avoid hitting a deer
   Keep a close watch for deer in the early morning and evening hours. Deer are most active during these times.
   Be especially alert and drive with caution when you are moving through a known deer crossing zone.
   Use your high-beam headlights whenever possible.
   Upon seeing a deer, immediately slow down. Do not swerve - because this can confuse deer as to where to
    run. It could also cause you to lose control and hit a tree or another car.
   Look for other deer after one has crossed the road. Deer are often found in herding packs.
   Always wear your seatbelt. Most people injured and/or killed in deer - automobile collisions were not wearing
    their seat belt.

    What to do when you Strike a Deer

    If you are unable to avoid a collision with a deer, take the following steps:

   Do not touch the animal! The deer, in attempting to move or get away, could hurt you or itself.
   Remove your automobile from the roadway, if possible.
   Call the police.
