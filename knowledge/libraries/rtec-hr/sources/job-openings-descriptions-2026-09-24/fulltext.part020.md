Identification of Alert Phases That Trigger Specific Action: Planning and response to a contagious virus
will depend on the risk involved. Unlike a catastrophic event such as an earthquake, a contagious virus
likely will have various phases in the lifecycle of its spread. Each phase will likely need a different response
strategy. For example, one phase might be the presence of unfounded fears and concern regarding a
certain virus, and the response might call for education and public awareness regarding the low risk
involved. Another phase, however, may be the onset of a full pandemic, which calls for extreme measures
to limit its spread. This CVRP shall be applicable to all contagious viruses however it is based on the phases
and sub-phases of a pandemic influenza as defined by the Centers for Disease Control (CDC) and the World
Health Organization (WHO). Table such as in the Appendices is beneficial to identify the phases and sub-
phases.

The World Health Organization (WHO) has identified six pandemic influenza alert phases. These WHO
phases have been universally accepted by most health departments and other government and private
organizations and shall be adopted and adapted by the transit agency.

Disinfection Program: Various contagious viruses can survive on surface materials for several days or
longer. Consequently, a transit agency shall work with local health authorities to determine a practical
response and consideration given to the resources required and the availability of those resources. RTEC
realizes that access to resources (such as antibacterial soap, masks, gloves, etc.) may be limited or severely
inhibited due to shortages and manufacturing shortfalls. EMS and health workers may also be given
priority access to such resources by the government and CDC.

Disinfect surfaces that may be contaminated with germs after cleaning: See a list of products with EPA-
approved emerging viral pathogens claims maintained by the American Chemistry Council Center for
Biocide Chemistries (CBC): https://www.americanchemistry.com/Novel-Coronavirus-Fighting-Products-
List.pdf

Products with EPA-approved emerging viral pathogens claims are expected to be effective against the
virus that causes COVID-19 based on data for harder to kill viruses. Follow the manufacturer’s instructions
for all cleaning and disinfection products (e.g., concentration, application method and contact time, and
use of personal protective equipment). Labels contain instructions for safe and effective use of the
cleaning product including precautions you should take when applying the product, such as wearing gloves
and making sure you have good ventilation during use of the product.

Practice routine cleaning of frequently touched surfaces: including tables, doorknobs, light switches,
handles, desks, toilets, faucets, sinks, etc. Clean and disinfect vehicles (inside surfaces, door handles, rails,
etc.) and office equipment (e.g., copy machines, phones, laptops, tablets, keyboards, etc.) routinely using
products with the EPA-approved emerging viral pathogens claims. Follow the manufacturer’s instructions
for all cleaning and disinfection products. Consult with the manufacturer about guidance on products
appropriate for disinfecting as necessary. If no guidance is available, consider the use of alcohol-based
wipes or spray containing at least 70% alcohol.

Practice hand hygiene frequently: wash hands often with soap and water for at least 20 seconds. If soap
and water are not readily available, use an alcohol-based hand sanitizer that contains at least 60% alcohol.

BLACK SKY EVENT
RTEC realizes that operations may be affected in an instance of large-scale catastrophe referred to as the
“Black Sky events” including extreme weather, mega earthquakes, cyber terrorism, high altitude
electromagnetic pulse, Intentional Electromagnetic Interference (EMP), and coordinated physical assault
that severely disrupts the normal functioning of our critical infrastructures in multiple regions for long
periods of time. In short, when the power goes out, eventually everything else we depend on will fail
including Oil, Gas, Water and Waste Management. Transportation limitations and supply chain
disruptions will make it difficult to open shelters, prepare and distribute food and emergency supplies,
and provide means for reunification services. Furthermore, if security is not addressed, unauthorized
tampering with supplies, crime, and looting could lead to even more critical disruption. Members of the
U.S. military, National Guard Adjutant Generals, officials from Homeland Security, Department of Energy,
FEMA, NOAA, and experts from the utility, distribution and supply sectors are in the process of planning
and preparing for such events to the extent possible to lessen the impact and improve recovery for such
events. Recent government planning exercises have reportedly included widespread long duration power
outages with no solution, critical decisions, assessments, actions and cross-sector coordination, with the
final phase being a “Lessons Imagined” assessment and feedback. RTEC will monitor feedback provided
to the public about the lessons they learned from these planning meetings and exercises and integrate
recommendations provided into our planning activities. RTEC will help in any way possible to improve
community resilience to large-scale events.

RTEC has already taken appropriate steps to contend with short-term power loss including (but not limited
to) equipping critical building infrastructures with Three (3) generators to provide temporary electrical
power. This will afford RTEC the ability to continue operation immediately after a power failure and give
time for further planning action as necessary for any long-term events. Conducting resupply operations
over a large-scale disrupted transportation network may prove difficult with fuel and supply shortages.
Partnerships (for local deployment of adequate fuel and supplies) with other critical users/sectors
(emergency management, hospitals, etc.) may present opportunities for facility sharing and mutual-
assistance agreements on fuel and resupply. These shortages will not only affect RTEC Operations, but
also our staff’s ability to get to and from work and obtain basic needs for their families (food, water, fuel,
supplies, etc.). Government restrictions may also be in place affecting service capabilities and provisions.
RTEC may have to minimize the number of vehicles in service to compensate for supply shortages
(workforce and equipment) and use designated emergency routes to access urgent transportation
destinations. RTEC may participate in carpooling, pre-planning, and coordination with DES and DOT to
amplify mutual aid.

RTEC will work closely with local health departments and government officials and representatives from
Emergency Management adhering to any recommendations and guidance. Existing RTEC staff will
monitor employee absences and adjust operating procedures as necessary to cope. Priority will be to
deploy personnel to Operational locations using surviving means of transportation and establishing
communications with a baseline from which to commit support assets when and where required. Service
Cancellations may prove inevitable in the long-term. RTEC strives to keep service interruption limited.
However, RTEC understands that the amount of service interruption will depend greatly on the severity
and scope of an event and the capability of the U.S. government to appropriately prepare and respond.
RTEC believes and supports that sustaining critical transportation during such a wide-spread “Black Sky
Event” will significantly aid in reducing loss of life and expedite recovery.

EVACUATION OF PASSENGERS FROM VEHICLE

PASSENGER EVACUATION TRAINING MANUAL

This training manual is designed to assist Drivers in making critical decisions in the event of an emergency
situation where vehicle Evacuation is necessary. It is not meant to replace existing policies or procedures,
but is meant to be a supplemental guide to help you protect your passengers, yourself and others. If you
are unsure of any policies and procedures you should contact your immediate supervisor.

Reasons For Actual Emergency Evacuations
1. Fire or danger of fire: Being near an existing fire and unable to move the vehicle, or being near the
presence of gasoline or other combustible material is considered dangerous and passengers should be
evacuated. The vehicle should be stopped and evacuated immediately if the engine or any other portion
of the vehicle is on fire. Passengers should be moved to a safe place 100 feet or more from the vehicle.

2. Unsafe position: When the bus is stopped because of an accident, mechanical failure, road conditions,
or human failure, the driver must determine immediately whether it is safer for passengers to remain on
the vehicle or to evacuate the vehicle. (Always try to exit on side AWAY from traffic.)

3. Sight distance: In normal traffic conditions, the vehicle should be visible for a distance of 300 feet or
more. A position that is over a hill or around a curve where such visibility does not exist should be
considered reason for evacuation. Ensure your vehicle is equipped with Triangle Road Reflectors.

4. Mandatory evacuations: The driver must evacuate the vehicle when:
   a. The final stopping point is in the path of a train. (Never assume no train will come.) If a train

Communicating with Passengers and Helpers
Being well trained in how to deal with evacuation emergencies will make it easier for you to remain calm.
Remaining calm is crucial! Time and conditions permitting, tell the passengers in a calm, clear and concise
manner that there is an emergency. Keep the situation as orderly and low key as possible. An explanation
of what they are required to do will help to prevent passenger hysteria. Passengers should be advised
that help is on the way, but for their safety it is best they leave and/or be assisted from the vehicle.
Encourage passengers to adopt a "buddy" for shared support through the emergency. Consider each
passenger’s abilities and determine who can exit the bus by themselves and who needs assistance.
Continued reassurance to passengers while performing your duties will also be helpful in forestalling any
panic. The use of able-bodied passengers or passersby must be done with great care. The ability to remain
calm and give clear and concise instructions to helpers will help prevent unnecessary injuries. Make it
clear what commands will be used to start whatever you will be doing. For example, if you’re planning to
take a particular action “on a count of 3”, your helper is better able to synchronize his or her actions with
you, rather than just using "GO" or some other single command. BE CALM!

Steps of the Evacuation Process
In most instances evacuation can be reduced to the following steps:

  1. Stop as soon as possible in a safe place. Secure the vehicle by putting the transmission in PARK and
     set the emergency brake. Turn the ignition off and take the keys.
  2. If time and conditions permit, the vehicle driver should use the communication system to advise
     the appropriate emergency personnel and dispatch of the following: a.) the exact location,
     including the nearest intersecting road or familiar landmark; b.) the condition creating the
     emergency; c.) the type of assistance needed (police, fire, ambulance); d.) notification that the bus
     is being evacuated. If there is a fire hazard, you should consider hanging the radio mike out the
     driver-side window so that once evacuation is complete radio communication may still be accessed
     outside the of the vehicle if safe.
  3. Release passengers from their vehicle restraints or seat belts by unbuckling or cutting. (If time is
     not limited and there is no immediate danger, use the wheelchair lift or ramp if accessible. If time
     IS limited, there is immediate danger, and there are passengers in a wheelchair, do not waste time
     unbuckling or releasing the wheelchair securement system -- instead, first remove the passenger,
     then if time permits recover the wheelchair. See Wheelchair Considerations Section of this training
     manual.)

Wheelchairs and Similar Mobility Device Considerations
When transporting a group of passengers on routes or agency group outings, it is imperative that the
emergency egress aisles are NOT TO BE BLOCKED. The driver must keep the van aisles clear at all times.
If a wheelchair user transfers to an ambulatory seat, the driver must properly secure the mobility device.
Unoccupied wheelchairs and mobility devices carried in the main body of the vehicle must be firmly
secured before the vehicle is in motion, and positioned so that they do not create an impediment or a
hazard to passengers. The aisles must remain clear and free of obstructions to allow ease of
maneuverability to vehicle emergency exits.
In the event that emergency evacuation of the vehicle is necessary, drivers or the emergency crew should
assist all passengers off the bus in the quickest, safest possible manner. If you are transporting wheelchair
users in a lift-equipped vehicle, the following methods are recommended in order of preference:

   1. Ask the person how you can help.

   2. If possible, use the passenger lift. Even in a fire emergency, it is the safest way for wheelchair users
   to evacuate a vehicle. Be aware of Manual Lift Operations in case of lift power failure. Refer to
   manufacturer instructions/guidelines of Manual Lift Operation before an accident or emergency
   situation occurs.
   3. If the lift is not operable in the usual way, but the vehicle has a relatively low floor, the driver may
   position the lift halfway between ground and floor to use as a step in emergencies. In this case, bring
   the passenger in the wheelchair out backwards. (Only as a last resort in emergency situations.)

   4. If the lift malfunctions or is inaccessible and another person is available to help, they can lift the
   passenger still seated in the wheelchair, backward through the vehicle rear entry door if equipped.

   5. If no other help is available, or the wheelchair will not release from its restraints, the driver must
   carry the passenger from the vehicle. Check with the passenger how best to carry them. In this
   situation there are several possible techniques to get passengers with special needs off the vehicle.
   One method is the one or two-person lift techniques. One person could drape their arms around your
   shoulders and with their face to their back and carry them to safety if able. Two or more persons could
   share the weight and carry the person to safety. If no one is available to help and the person is too

Persons With Special Needs:
Visual Impaired: In an emergency, tell the person the nature of the emergency and offer to guide him/her.
As you walk, tell the person where you are and advise of any obstacles. When you have reached safety,
orient the person to where he/she is and ask if any further assistance is needed.

Hearing Impaired: Persons with impaired hearing may not perceive emergency alarms and an alternative
warning technique is required. Warning methods may include writing a note about the emergency and
evacuation route or turning the light switch on and off. Then indicate through gestures or writing what is
happening and what to do.

Persons Using Crutches, Canes, or Walkers: Ask if the person needs assistance to evacuate. Offer to guide
him/her to the emergency exit. Carrying or dragging the person to safety may be necessary.

Learning Disabilities: Assist persons by giving them directions and orienting them to the emergency exits.

We provide reasonable modification of policy and practice upon request to ensure that our transportation

     Able to walk without assistance;
     Able to walk with the use of a cane, a walker, or crutches;

   Visually impaired or blind;
   Hearing impaired or deaf;
   Speech impaired;

performing any of the following:
     Going up or down stairs, inclines, or ladders;
     Stooping, kneeling, or crouching;
     Walking long distances, even at a very slow pace;

       Waiting or standing;

       Moving dynamically (i.e., running, jumping, or reacting instantly);
       Moving within confined spaces;
       Establishing accurate depth perception; and
       Communicating (i.e., understanding instructions and making their own needs           understood).

Although many elderly and disabled passengers may have little difficulty using rail transit under normal
circumstances, the limitations listed above may prevent them from moving with the speed, agility, and
sureness needed to evacuate a transit vehicle. The ability of passengers to hear, read, and understand
instructions, or make themselves heard and understood during rail transit emergencies will affect
response operations. For example, the inability of a passenger to understand an instruction because of a
hearing or visual impairment could make it difficult to move that person to safety. Such impairment may
also make it difficult for these passengers to remain calm and confident because of a feeling that they are
trapped or will not receive help. The key point in communication is whether passengers are able to
understand oral and written instructions and are then able to follow directions. During an emergency,
elderly and disabled passengers may not be able to do any of the following:
     Understand that there is an emergency requiring evacuation from the area;
     Read and understand signs or instructions; and
     Hear and understand instructions.

Many medical, physical, and mental conditions do not significantly limit the functional capabilities of
elderly and disabled persons or may pose only temporary limitations, and may not be perceptible to an
outside observer. Medical conditions which may not be continuously disabling include epilepsy, diabetes,
asthma and other respiratory ailments, arthritis, and heart disease. Physical conditions include back or
spinal conditions, degenerative muscle or bone conditions, cerebral palsy, nervous disorders, and missing
or paralyzed limbs. Mental conditions include, memory loss, senility, retardation, and various degrees of
psychological and psychiatric disorders, such as schizophrenia.

Under normal circumstances, transit passengers with medical conditions such as epilepsy and diabetes or
who have cardiovascular, respiratory, or other impairments do not usually require special assistance while
using transit facilities and vehicles. However, a stroke or heart attack, asthma flare-up, fainting, epileptic
episode, a fall, etc. could in and of themselves be considered an emergency, requiring medical assistance
and evacuation. Because some passenger limitations are not readily apparent, transit or emergency
response personnel or other passengers may not recognize the immediate or potential need for special
assistance to elderly or disabled passengers. Moreover, the lack of medication due to time delays and
lack of medical assistance from those knowledgeable of a given condition or disability may cause or
exaggerate conditions. In addition, elderly or disabled passengers may be more prone to injury from falls.

In summary, passengers with medical, physical, and mental conditions may be unable to do any of the
following:
      Prevent the onset of adverse health conditions such as heart attacks, angina, shortness of breath,

The purpose of this training is to train and inform employees in compliance with the OSHA Hazard
Communication Standard, 29 CFR 1910.1200, as amended and as adopted by 803 KAR 2:320, by compiling
a hazardous chemicals list, by using material safety data sheets (SDS), by ensuring that containers are
labeled, and promoting a safe working environment. OSHA revised its Hazard Communication Standard
(HCS) to align with the United Nations’ Globally Harmonized System of Classification and Labeling of
Chemicals (GHS). Two significant changes contained in the revised standard require the use of new
labeling elements and a standardized format for Safety Data Sheets (SDSs), formerly known as, Material
Safety Data Sheets (MSDSs). RTEC’s Hazardous Communication program applies to all work operations in
our company where employees may be exposed to hazardous substances under normal working
conditions or during an emergency situation. Not all chemicals utilized by Rural Transit Enterprises
Coordinated (RTEC) employees may be classified as “hazardous”, but awareness training will be available
nonetheless. Under this program employees are informed of the contents of the Hazard Communication
Standard, the hazardous properties of chemicals with which they work, safe handling procedures, and
measures to take affording appropriate protection to themselves and those around them from these
chemicals or materials. Personal Protective Equipment training and policies and procedures are also
covered as necessary. The training emphasizes these items:

             Summary of the standard and this written program;
