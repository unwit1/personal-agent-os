Annual Review, Update, and Evaluation
RTEC strives to improve the quality of the Transit Agency Safety Plan. It is a living document and subject
to change, growth, and development as necessary. Administration and department managers should
periodically evaluate the plan and suggest changes as necessary to the Plan Coordinator. Deficiencies and
identification of what aspects of the program need improvement should be reported immediately to the
Coordinator. RTEC Administration will evaluate the Transit Agency Safety Plan in any of several ways,
some of which are described below:

       Set measurable, formal goals for the program and measure progress toward meeting them. This
        usually involves “benchmarking,” or comparing measures of performance (such as accident or
        incident rates) against a base line of other similarly sized companies or top performers elsewhere.
        Comparing the program’s results in a given year to earlier year’s may yield information on the
        program’s progress. External factors beyond company control may negatively affect the ability
        to forecast or interpret data. RTEC may utilize any available resources for benchmarking.
       Analyze Safety enhancement programs.
       Review Documentation and Reports (Such as Accident, Injury Reports, equipment breakdowns
        and repair reports) and look for any patterns.
       Set standards for various tasks performed by employees and include them in Policy updates and
        Safety Notices. Once employees understand the standards, managers can evaluate employee’s
        completed work to measure how well it meets the standards.

Corrective Actions and changes will be made to the plan as conditions arise that require it or areas of
improvement are identified. RTEC will continue to ensure that Federally and State funded Equipment and
Buildings are maintained and kept in good working order for as long as is feasible. RTEC will review this
plan at a minimum annually and update it with appropriate changes and additions.

Safety Performance Targets

Risk – The chance of a bad consequence.
Likelihood – The chance that an event or behavior may occur.
Severity – The level of harm that may occur.

The ability to effectively assess risk regarding physical risk behavior is essential to critical decision making
during a crisis event. Developing critical decision-making skills enables control of emotions and behaviors
in order to make appropriate judgments about the necessary actions required to minimize risk. There
exists a good deal of common-sense knowledge on many types of risks, from the most to the least
conspicuous. These labels are not absolutes, but they are there to give guidance when making decisions
about likelihood and severity of harm that can occur. These tools may prove useful during the planning
and decision making process to help ensure the Safety and Security of everyone involved in the daily
transportation services RTEC provides.

RTEC Safety Policy Statement

Rural Transit Enterprises Coordinated, Inc. has established this Fleet Safety Policy Statement to emphasize
its overall commitment to the safety of our passengers, our drivers, our staff, and the general public.

Rural Transit Enterprises Coordinated, Inc. recognizes the need for continuous improvement and
dedication to safety enhancement in the work environment. Accidents cause suffering to our employees,
customers, and the general public. They impact the company through direct losses or increased operation
costs. By establishing and implementing this Safety Policy we can and will greatly reduce or eliminate
accidents and have a positive impact on our operations.

Rural Transit Enterprises Coordinated, Inc. Administration and Management will be responsible for
implementing and coordinating the elements of the company’s Safety Policies and Practices. All
employees of Rural Transit Enterprises Coordinated, Inc. play an important role in the safety and success
of our endeavors. For the protection and conservation of the well being for everyone involved, “Safety
must be the first thought in everything we do”.

RTEC believes its Transit Agency Safety Plan meets or exceeds the following MAP-21 Safety Authority

RISK MANAGEMENT

RTEC has its own policies and procedures. Policies and Procedure Manuals and Plans, including the
Employee Handbook are available for review on the Employee Portal Page at www.ridertec.org.
Employees are responsible for reading and adhering to all policies and procedures.

RTEC maintains $5,000,000.00 insurance coverage for vehicles and property damage. RTEC would qualify
for higher limits, if necessary. Moreover, RTEC maintains a tracking database specifically designed to track
annually renewable certificates such as vehicle insurance, workers’ compensation insurance, operating
authority, fee receipts and Medicaid provider numbers for subcontractors. This enables us to send
reminders to subcontractors when renewal is required. The Subcontractors will continue to be required
to maintain proper and sufficient insurance certificates and meet qualification standards, further reducing
the risks to the Commonwealth, RTEC and the recipients served under this project. Notice of cancellation
would be given to RTEC in the event a subcontractor’s insurance is cancelled for any reason.

RTEC also tracks foster parent and private auto provider information as well, including driver license,
vehicle registration and proof of insurance and sends out notices monthly to providers whose information
is soon to expire that renewal is necessary. In addition, RTEC performs criminal background checks on all
private auto provider applicants, while the Cabinet for Health & Family Services conducts background
checks on all foster parent providers.

RTEC first believes in reducing business risks through prevention using effective training, policy and
procedures. We must still be prepared for emergencies, and these of course include the non-preventable
natural disasters. For this latter category, our Transit Agency Safety Plan includes our Safety/Security,
Emergency Preparedness, and Disaster Recovery plans, providing a base line of procedures for managing
the emergency prevention and response process and reducing business risks. This plan is available to the
public upon request. RTEC designated employees also regularly participate in local disaster preparedness
planning and meetings. RTEC stays involved with organizations such as the Lake Cumberland Emergency
Preparedness Committee (LEPC) and participates in local planning and drill exercises.

RTEC’s qualified personnel are prepared to handle emergencies in the office and have in place back-up
procedures for revamping, relocating to a new location if necessary, and recovering data that is backed-
up and stored in a safety deposit box securely at RTEC’s bank. To strengthen RTEC’s security and to further
efforts to avert security threats, all outside entryways to RTEC’s main building have been equipped with
security systems and all staff issued personalized name badges that must be scanned for entry. Main
buildings and vehicles are equipped with Video Surveillance technology. Safety-sensitive employees
receive additional safety and security training as applicable to Region 12 transportation services. RTEC
also provides training to subcontractors’ Drug & Alcohol Program Managers stressing the importance of
contemporaneous, articulable and specific documentation prior to any implementation of reasonable
suspicion testing, thereby curtailing substance abuse and reducing the risk of avoidable legal issues.

Safety sensitive employees and Subcontracted Drivers and Dispatchers participate in the required Drug
and Alcohol Testing Program. These employees are subject to pre-employment, random, post-accident,
and reasonable suspicion testing as required by KYTC to ensure a drug and alcohol free workplace. RTEC
operates a drug and alcohol free zero tolerance workplace and all employees certify to the requirement
that no use or dispensing thereof is tolerated.

Objectives of Maintenance
The primary objective of the maintenance program is to have optimal operations running capability with
a vigilant eye on proper conservation of energy and workforce. Corrective maintenance and preventive
maintenance during the year life expectancy of the facilities can be performed to prevent future cost
and/or shutdowns. Repairs or replacements are necessary to maintain the buildings, grounds, and fixed
equipment in an operable condition. Specifically this can be further broken down as follows:

1. To provide buildings which function at optimal efficiency.
2. To maintain the buildings and grounds and fixed equipment in such a manner as to eliminate or reduce
to acceptable levels, fires, accidents, and safety hazards.
3. To provide continuous use of facilities without disruptions to the transportation programs.
4. To protect public property by planned, scheduled, and repair maintenance.
5. To conserve energy by ensuring that the maximum results are obtained with a minimal expenditure of
energy.
6. To provide a maintenance program that will produce the maximum amount of maintenance for the
dollars expended.
7. When questions regarding environmental issues are presented, the appropriate maintenance
authorities and workers and/or appropriate officials are contacted to perform and to subsequently
monitor issues that may emanate from specific problems. Reports and findings are returned to RTEC and
corrective measures are taken, if so warranted.

Vehicle Maintenance
An effective preventative maintenance program and good record keeping system is as important to a
successful transportation system as the purchase of the vehicles. Rather than waiting until something has
been put in place to keep repair costs at a minimum and extend the useful life of the vehicle. The driver
plays a major role in the safety and preventive maintenance program by conducting the Pre-Trip vehicle
inspection each day prior to the operation of the vehicle and also with a Post-Trip vehicle inspection. The
inspections are to be completed thoroughly and promptly with any unsafe conditions to be reported to
the supervisor, immediately. Being responsible for the systematic daily inspection of the vehicle, the
driver must have full knowledge of the maintenance program in place. Having these responsibilities will
ensure that the driver knows when it is time for the scheduled maintenance to be performed.

RTEC has prepared a Vehicle Maintenance Program Manual as a guideline for vehicle operators and
maintenance personnel which discusses the correct procedures for conducting preventative
maintenance, the daily vehicle inspections, vehicle accident procedures, communications system use, and
reporting procedures. Mechanics assure that the minimum standards required for the transit system’s
vehicles are met. Certain maintenance requirements vary for each type of vehicle. The maintenance
program is subject to change and update as necessary.

Safety Assurance

Monitoring and Measurement:
RTEC will monitor the system for compliance with procedures for operations and maintenance. Safety
assurance provides the necessary feedback to ensure that RTEC is functioning effectively and meeting or
exceeding its safety objectives. Everyone in the organization takes an active role in securing transit safety.
Regular communications, transit meetings, and training sessions comprised of staff at varying levels,
including executives, officers, managers, operators, and maintenance personnel are routinely held.

RTEC is committed to using the data collected and information learned to inform decision making and
instill positive change. RTEC will strive for continuous improvement of transit system safety. When
performance goals are not met, RTEC will work to identify why such goals were not met and what actions
can be taken to minimize the gap in achieving the desired goal or outcome. However, when goals are
easily achieved, action will be taken to exceed expectations and re-establish a reasonable baseline for
measurement. Factors outside RTEC’s control will be taken into consideration.

Uses of performance results include:
   - Focus attention on performance weaknesses or gaps.
   - Investigate problem areas.
   - Help make informed decisions and allocate appropriate resources.
   - Help motivate employees to continue making program improvements wherever feasible.
   - Identify needs for Staff training or technical assistance.
   - Identify best practices through benchmarking with other similar agencies.
   - Strategic planning efforts and use of industry resources.
   - Respond to governing entities and the public’s demand for accountability.

RTEC will conduct investigations of safety events and examine causal factors considering preventability
and accountability. Every effort will be made to learn from past mistakes to prevent reoccurrence and
minimize risk as much as is feasible in all aspects of the services we provide.

Any information reported through internal safety reporting programs or voluntary employee reporting
will be taken seriously and examined for plans of correction or action needed to mitigate problem areas.
Employees are encouraged to make reports of any safety concerns to RTEC administration without fear
of punishment or reprisal.

RTEC will continue to invest in developing skilled human resources essential to sustaining a safety
management system. Both technical and managerial skills will be needed for data collection and analysis,
and achieving goals. Managing staff and the governing board will commit the financial resources required
for organizational capacity and maintaining a culture of safety on a continuous basis.

Traffic Accident Review Procedures
All traffic accidents involving RTEC vehicles are reviewed by the RTEC Accident Review Committee.
(Comprised of three or more individuals selected by RTEC Management). In an effort to reduce risk and
to improve safety, professionalism and consistency in accident review, RTEC has a more objective process
for handling discipline for at-fault accidents through a point system. The point system is an on-going
process to monitor performance and ensure drivers are treated fairly and consistently. Drivers accumulate
disciplinary points for at-fault accidents/incidents and for negative activities management deems the
point system as an appropriate disciplinary measure. Extraordinary circumstances are unique and may
warrant more progressive discipline. (Note: this accident/incident point accrual system is separate from
any attendance point accrual system – but may be used conjunctively during performance review and
disciplinary measure determination). Refer to RTEC’s Driver Handbook for more information.

SCOPE
This process covers the contingency management-planning phase of Rural Transit Enterprises
Coordinated, Inc. (RTEC). Throughout the recovery effort, the plan establishes clear lines of authority and

       Provide for the safety and well-being of people on the premises at the time of a disaster and

       Establish management succession and emergency powers;

WHEN A DISASTER IS RECOGNIZED
In the event of a disaster, the Incident Commander and the disaster recovery coordinator will be
contacted. The coordinator will contact the emergency management team. The emergency management
team will go to the area of the disaster, assess the damage and provide the Incident Commander and the
coordinator with the results of the assessment as soon as possible. The Incident Commander and the
disaster recovery coordinator will decide which other teams to contact depending on the type and severity
of the disaster. Disaster recovery operations will not begin until the Incident Commander and the
Coordinator designate an operation plan. Department Managers and administrative staff are empowered
to maintain control of their departments and minimize service disruption while recovery planning and
organization are underway. (Contact Insurance Carrier KACo Claims for aide 1-866-367-5226)

SUSPICIOUS PACKAGES AND DEVICES

Suspicion is based upon the type and location of the package. NOT all lost and found items are suspicious
packages. Usually those items left in conspicuous areas such as on seats, in a restroom, next to a phone
booth or vending machine are simply forgotten items. Reminding disembarking passengers to ensure
they have all of their belongings before leaving will greatly reduce lost or left-behind items.

Signs for Concern: A package that is left or intentionally placed in an out-of-the-way location where it is
not readily visible; matches something described in a threat or has a threatening note attached; is an
abandoned item or container such as a thermos, propane canister, fire extinguisher or piece of pipe; has
visible wires, batteries, a clock or timer, or has bottles, tanks or bags attached; is abandoned by someone
quickly leaving the area; includes a bag, box or package emitting an odor, mist or oily liquid; is a bottle
filled with unusual colored liquid or has strange objects inside.

Responses:
       DO NOT use a radio or cell phone if an explosive device is suspected.
       Make notification through appropriate channels – Call 9-1-1.
       Give a description of the package or device and its location.
       DO NOT touch, move or cover the object.
       If there is immediate danger, remain calm and evacuate the area safely.
       Attempt to isolate and secure the area.
       DO NOT re-enter once everyone is out.
       Await direction from supervisor or emergency responders.

As a transit employee you may encounter people who are “different”, “difficult”, and possibly even
“dangerous”. The key to dealing effectively with all these types of people is to maintain self-control and
strive to communicate effectively. Respecting others’ differences is an important component of
managing your behavior. The ability to understand and respect all people regardless of their culture,
background, physical appearance, thoughts, ideas, expressions, attitudes and beliefs plays a key role in
managing your everyday interactions with people. Treat others with dignity. Don’t act upon assumptions
and stereotypes.

Defusing the situation may involve using communication skills to engage the difficult person and help
resolve or reduce the conflict. Do not try to bargain with a person or use defusing strategies if he/she
becomes threatening. If involved with a threatening or dangerous person, seek immediate assistance
from the proper authorities. Here are some tips for defusing incidents:

     Respect All Individuals’ Personal Space. Personal Space is the area around us that we consider an
      extension of ourselves. How much space each of us requires to feel comfortable varies
      considerably. What is almost universally true, however, is that anxiety rises when personal space
      is invaded. This heightened anxiety makes it more likely a person will act out in a more serious
      way. To avoid such a response, maintain at least an arm’s-length distance from a person whose
      behavior is escalating. In this way, you will be less likely to increase the person’s anxiety. You will
      also reduce danger to yourself by maintaining a margin of safety that allows you time to react if
      the person becomes physically aggressive.

     Be Aware of Your Own Body Position. In addition to maintaining adequate space between you
      and an anxious person, avoid eye-to-eye, toe-to-toe positions, as they might be interpreted as
      challenging. Positions perceived as challenging can evoke a “fight or flight” response from the
      other person, and neither of those reactions is likely to be helpful. Standing at an angle to the
      person and off to the side is much less likely to escalate an agitated person’s behavior.

     Acknowledge the persons’ feelings. Try not to judge or discount the feelings of others. Whether
      or not you think their feelings are justified, those feelings are real to the other person. Pay
      attention to them, and don’t be afraid of silence. Your supportive presence is often more
      important than what could be conveyed with any words you might say.

     Keep Nonverbal Cues Nonthreatening. As a person begins to lose control of rational thought, the
      person becomes more tuned into your body language and less tuned into your words. Nonverbal
      communication – including gestures, facial expressions, movements and tone of voice – becomes
      paramount in conveying a calm, respectful attitude.

     Ignore Challenging Questions. When a person challenges your authority or an organizational
      policy, redirect the individual’s attention to the issue at hand. For example, suppose a female

      person to release as much energy as possible by venting verbally. Verbal venting is always safer
      than physical venting. As a person is venting, there will be peaks and valleys in the outburst, as
      the person’s expenditure rises and falls. If you cannot allow the person to continue venting, state
      the directives and reasonable limits during the “valleys” in the venting process.

     Stay Composed, Avoid Overreacting. Try to identify the real reason for the behavior. Even in the
      midst of an angry outburst, there is useful information to be gained about what a person is
      thinking and feeling. Try to listen for the real message – the feelings behind the facts. Restate
      the message you think you have received in order to determine if you correctly understood the
      persons’ intent. It’s hard not to take things personally, especially since angry people often say
      very personal things. But it is essential to do your best to remain calm and professional – at least
      on the outside. Your composed, rational response can go a long way toward influencing the
      person’s behavior in a positive way.

     Prevention Promotes Culture of Caring. Not every crisis situation can be successfully de-
      escalated, but staff members who know these key principles are much more likely to influence
      behavior in a positive way, defusing potential crisis situations BEFORE they become dangerous.
      PREVENTION is the best way to promote a culture of caring and a safe and respectful workplace
      for everyone.

BEHAVIOR MANAGEMENT ON THE RTEC VEHICLE

Behavior is something that can be observed and it can be inwardly or outwardly directed. Referring to
social and emotional disabilities, some behaviors can be disturbing to others and the environment. When
in-appropriate behavior occurs on the RTEC bus, you must do something to stop or change the behavior.
You can gain control of the situation by directing a rider’s undesirable behavior away from riders toward
you as the receiver of that behavior. It is usually helpful to attempt to determine the cause of the behavior
and who the behavior is being directed toward. One way to discourage undesirable behavior is to ignore
it. However, if you choose this method, you should be aware of the following potential circumstances:

     The behavior will get worse before it gets better. You need to be sure you can wait it out.
     It is important that everyone ignore the behavior before it will go away. If you ignore a riders’
      antics and the rest of the riders laugh, this method will not work.

Inwardly Directed behaviors may not involve other people or the environment, but can also be
inappropriate or disruptive. It may appear that the client is unaware of his/her surroundings. The person
may talk to themselves or repeat certain phrases over and over. Do not become alarmed and remain
calm. The rider may be repeating phrases from conversations that were overheard or a catchy phrase
picked up from his/her environment before you arrived to pick him/her up. It is possible that the rider’s
strange talk is self-motivated. That is, the rider created it in his/her own mind for his/her own amusement.
Inwardly directed behavior can be more difficult to manage since the driver will not be able to redirect
the behavior toward him/herself. Do not worry too much about a client’s inwardly directed behavior
unless the client becomes significantly agitated and/or becomes self-destructive. In this instance, it is
important to notify the serving agency or the family as soon as possible and let them know what behavior
you are seeing in the client. If behavior becomes an immediate danger to self or others, calling
appropriate authorities may be necessary (i.e. 9-1-1).

The environment can also have a profound effect on one’s behavior. When a client gets on the van in the
morning, he/she may have come from a situation that was very disturbing. Perhaps there was a fight or,
maybe the client did not get to wear a favorite outfit. The client will bring that problem onto the van. By
being alert and paying attention to the riders’ moods, a driver will be able to alert caretakers to potential
problems both at home and away from the home. Driver’s should report any signs of abuse/neglect to
appropriate authorities immediately; the Abuse & Neglect Statewide Report Number is 877-KY SAFE1.

The atmosphere of a vehicle can affect the behavior of riders. Drivers should keep their vans clean and in
good shape as this sends a message to riders about the driver’s responsive attitude. Riders will come to
appreciate the cleanliness and comfort of a van that is kept free of papers and garbage. Vans need to be
swept and cleaned out frequently. Also remember that the temperature, the loudness and type of music
played will affect rider’s behavior. Use of “Physical Restraint” of RTEC’s clients is NOT permitted under
RTEC Policy/Procedures. Do no harm. Report any signs of abuse/neglect to appropriate authorities
immediately.

Finally, it is important to remember that you are a part of the environment of your van. If you are cheerful,
chances are that your riders will be cheerful too. How you talk with them and respond to their needs tells
them whether or not your van is a safe and comfortable place to travel.

HUMAN TRAFFICKING AWARENESS AND PUBLIC SAFETY INITIATIVE

Human trafficking is a modern form of slavery, with nearly 25 million victims worldwide, including in the

trafficking. DOT's Transportation Leaders Against Human Trafficking (TLAHT) initiative was formed in 2012
for transportation and travel industry stakeholders to maximize the transportation industry collective
impact on human trafficking. TLAHT’s focus areas are leadership, training and education, policy
development, public awareness, and information sharing and analysis. Partners take action to combat
human trafficking by issuing leadership statements, signing pledges, developing reporting protocols,

ACTIVE SHOOTER

Here is what to do if you find yourself in an active shooting event, how to recognize signs of potential
violence around you, and what to expect after an active shooting takes place. If you see something, say
something to an authority right away. Be aware of your environment and any possible dangers. Look for
the two nearest exits anywhere you go, and have an escape path in mind & identify places you could hide.
Remember during an active shooting to RUN. HIDE. FIGHT.

During:
RUN and escape, if possible.
   - Getting away from the shooter or shooters is the top priority.
   - Leave your belongings behind and get away.
   - Help others escape, if possible, but evacuate regardless of whether others agree to follow.
   - Warn and prevent individuals from entering an area where the active shooter may be.
   - Call 911 when you are safe, and describe shooter, location, and weapons.
HIDE, if escape is not possible.
   - Get out of the shooter’s view and stay very quiet.
   - Silence all electronic devices and make sure they won’t vibrate.
   - Lock and block doors, close blinds, and turn off lights.
   - Don’t hide in groups- spread out along walls or hide separately to make it more difficult for the
         shooter.
   - Try to communicate with police silently. Use text message or social media to tag your location,
         or put a sign in a window.
   - Stay in place until law enforcement gives you the all clear.
   - Your hiding place should be out of the shooter's view and provide protection if shots are fired in
         your direction.
FIGHT as an absolute last resort.
   - Commit to your actions and act as aggressively as possible against the shooter.
   - Recruit others to ambush the shooter with makeshift weapons like chairs, fire extinguishers,
         scissors, books, etc.
   - Be prepared to cause severe or lethal injury to the shooter.
   - Throw items and improvise weapons to distract and disarm the shooter.

SEVERE WEATHER PRECAUTIONS
Always monitor weather forecasts. A Weather Alert Radio should be and available in the Operations
Department and maintained by the Operations Manager and Dispatch Team. Do not attempt a scheduled
run if there is a severe weather condition creating clear and present danger. These guidelines have been
established:

Tornado: The safest place to be during a tornado is in a basement. Get under a workbench or other piece
of sturdy furniture. If no basement is available, seek shelter on the lowest floor of the building in an
interior hallway or room such as a closet. Use blankets or pillows to cover your body and always stay
away from windows. If in a vehicle, do not try to out run a tornado. In some cases it is possible to avoid
a tornado by driving a right angle to its path. If in mobile homes or vehicles, evacuate them and get inside
a substantial shelter. If no shelter is available, lie flat in the nearest ditch or other low spot and cover your
head with your hands.

Reminder to Dispatchers and Drivers: Vehicles are extremely risky in a tornado. There is no safe option
when caught in a tornado in a vehicle, just slightly less-dangerous ones. If the tornado is visible, far away,
and the traffic is light, you may be able to drive out of its path by moving at right angles to the tornado.
Seek shelter in a sturdy building, or underground if possible. If you are caught by extreme winds or flying
debris, park the vehicle as quickly and safely as possible -- out of the traffic lanes. Stay in the car with the
seat(s) belt on. Put your head down below the windows; cover your head with your hands and a blanket,
coat, or other cushion if possible. If you can safely get noticeably lower than the level of the roadway,
leave your car and lie in that area, covering your head with your hands.

Additional Tornado and Safety Information:
There is no such thing as guaranteed safety inside a tornado. Freak accidents happen; and the most
violent tornadoes can level and blow away almost any house and its occupants. Extremely violent EF5
tornadoes are very rare, though. Most tornadoes are actually much weaker and can be survived using
these safety ideas... Know where you can take shelter in a matter of seconds, and practice a family tornado
drill at least once a year. Have a pre-determined place to meet after a disaster. Flying debris is the
greatest danger in tornadoes; so store protective coverings (e.g., mattress, sleeping bags, thick blankets,
etc.) in or next to your shelter space, ready to use on a few seconds' notice. When a tornado watch is
issued, think about the drill and check to make sure all your safety supplies are handy. Turn on local TV,
radio or NOAA Weather Radio and stay alert for warnings. Forget about the old notion of opening
windows to equalize pressure; the tornado will blast open the windows for you! If you shop frequently
at certain stores, learn where there are bathrooms, storage rooms or other interior shelter areas away
from windows, and the shortest ways to get there. In an office building, go directly to an enclosed,
windowless area in the center of the building -- away from glass and on the lowest floor possible. Then,
crouch down and cover your head. Interior stairwells are usually good places to take shelter, and if not
crowded, allow you to get to a lower level quickly.

render aid to those who are injured. Stay away from power lines and puddles with wires in them; they
may still be carrying electricity! Watch your step to avoid broken glass, nails, and other sharp objects.
Stay out of any heavily damaged houses or buildings; they could collapse at any time.         Do not use

water, or it may cover a house to the rooftop. NEVER drive your vehicle through swiftly running water or
standing pools whose depth cannot be judged. A few inches of a strong current can undermine roadbeds,
as well as carry your vehicle off the roadway. If necessary, seek an alternate route. Contact Dispatch or
your supervisor for advice. Coordinate with Dispatch.
Snow and Ice: Listen to radio for current weather information before attempting a trip in the wintertime.
Follow the guidelines for trip cancellation as stated in the Inclement Weather Policy above. If you are
caught in a blizzard, take the following actions:
                A. Pull off the road and turn on four-way flashers.
                B. Radio or signal for help.
                C. Leave one or two windows slightly open.
                D. Run engine for short spurts.

Fog: Turn on the low beam of your headlights and slow down. Do not drive faster than the visibility
allows.

ADDITIONAL PRECAUTIONS: EARTHQUAKE
Earthquake: In the event an earthquake does occur, here are some of FEMA's recommendations for
surviving an earthquake:

       Drop: Drop wherever you are on to your hands and knees. If you’re using a wheelchair or walker
        with a seat, make sure your wheels are locked and remain seated until the shaking stops.
       Cover: Cover your head and neck with your arms. If a sturdy table or desk is nearby, crawl
        underneath it for shelter. If no shelter is nearby, crawl next to an interior wall (away from
        windows). Crawl only if you can reach better cover without going through an area with more
        debris. Stay on your knees or bent over to protect vital organs.
       Hold on: If you are under a table or desk, hold on with one hand and be ready to move with it if it
        moves. If you can’t find a table or desk, cover your head and neck with both arms and hands. If
        seated and unable to drop to the floor, bend forward, cover your head with your arms, and hold
        on to your neck with both hands.
If in a moving vehicle, Pull over to the side of the road and stop as quickly as safety permits and stay in
the vehicle. Avoid stopping near or under buildings, trees, overpasses, and utility wires. Guide Note: an
earthquake while you're driving feels like there's something wrong with your car. Don't stop in the middle
of the freeway if traffic is still moving around you. Slow down and put on your turn signal to get to the
side of the road. Proceed cautiously once the earthquake has stopped. Avoid roads, bridges, or ramps that
might have been damaged by the earthquake.
